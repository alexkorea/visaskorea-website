# Overseas Korean Visa (F-4): Eligibility and Application Guide

The F-4 visa is a residency status granted to ethnic Koreans holding foreign citizenship, allowing them to live and work freely in Korea.

It is open to applicants who previously held Korean nationality themselves, or whose parent or grandparent once held Korean nationality.

This guide covers eligibility, supporting documents, the application process, permitted activities, and the practical sticking points that often trip applicants up.

<h2 id="section-1">What Is the F-4 Visa?</h2>

### Legal Basis and Purpose

The F-4 is a residency status granted to overseas Koreans of foreign nationality under the Act on the Immigration and Legal Status of Overseas Koreans.

The full legal text is available at the [Korean Law Information Center](https://law.go.kr).

Holders may stay in Korea indefinitely, travel in and out freely, and engage in a wide range of employment activities.

The trickier piece is the restriction on simple-labor occupations.

### How It Differs from the H-2 Working Visit Visa

The H-2 ties the entry date tightly to visa issuance and caps total stay at 4 years and 10 months.

The F-4 effectively has no cap on stay length, and it also opens the door for accompanying spouse and children visas (F-1, F-3).

| Category | F-4 Overseas Korean | H-2 Working Visit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stay period | Renewed in 3-year blocks, effectively unlimited | Up to 4 years, 10 months |
| Simple labor | Restricted | Permitted (designated sectors) |
| Family accompaniment | Allowed (F-1) | Limited |
| Eligibility proof | Proof of Korean lineage | Lottery / training process |

<h2 id="section-2">F-4 Eligibility Essentials</h2>

### Who Can Apply

The first thing to check is whether you, or a direct ascendant, previously held Korean nationality.

- Individuals who held Korean nationality before birth and later acquired foreign citizenship
- Individuals with a parent or grandparent who once held Korean nationality
- Applicants aged 18 or older (minors apply jointly with a parent)

### The Core of Proving Overseas Korean Status

More important than the volume of paperwork is presenting an unbroken bloodline trail.

For ethnic Korean Chinese applicants, the household register (居民户口簿), kinship notarization, and birth notarization issued in China must all match the Korean family register and family relations records for the same person.

For CIS Koreans (Goryeo-saram), birth certificates from the country of birth and documentation of parents' nationality changes are also required.

If this piece is weak, no amount of supporting paperwork will usually clear the first review.

### Disqualifying Factors

- Serious criminal records such as narcotics, smuggling, or tax evasion
- Prior deportation (with re-entry ban still in effect)
- History of submitting falsified documents

> **Caution:** If you previously entered on an H-2 and worked outside permitted simple-labor sectors, or have any immigration law violations on record, they will surface during F-4 review. Referral to disciplinary screening is a real possibility, so a pre-review is the safer path.

<h2 id="section-3">Required Documents and How to Prove Eligibility</h2>

### Standard Application Documents

| Document | Details | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visa application form | Prescribed format | [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) form |
| Passport copy | Valid for 6+ months | Identification page |
| Standard-format photo | Taken within last 6 months | Must meet specifications |
| Overseas Korean proof | Home-country notarization + Korean family register match | Core documentation |
| Proof of residence | Korean address documentation | Lease agreement, etc. |

### Supporting Documents for Lineage Proof

- Korean removed family register or family relations certificate for the applicant or direct ascendant
- Home-country household register and kinship notarization (China)
- Birth certificate and parental nationality-change records (CIS region)
- Notarized name-change documentation if applicable

### Common Oversights

The documents most often missing are those linking back to the grandparent generation.

Filing with only the parents' household register leaves no direct link to the Korean family register.

In most cases, you must trace back to the grandfather's or great-grandfather's removed family register before the bloodline becomes clear.

Missing certified translations also get caught at exactly this stage.

<h2 id="section-4">Application Process and Processing Time</h2>

### Applying from Inside vs. Outside Korea

If you already hold another residency status in Korea, such as H-2, the process is a status-of-stay change application.

Those residing abroad apply for a visa at the Korean diplomatic mission in their home country before entering.

| Stage | What Happens | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Step 1 | Eligibility review and document preparation | Matching of lineage documents |
| Step 2 | Home-country notarization and translation, Korean document issuance | Apostille verification |
| Step 3 | Visa application or status change application | Consulate or Immigration Office |
| Step 4 | Review and disciplinary screening (if applicable) | Additional documents may be requested |
| Step 5 | Issuance and alien registration | Within 90 days of entry |

### Processing Time

Processing time varies by Immigration Office jurisdiction and shifts again depending on whether the case is referred to disciplinary screening.

The fastest jurisdictions and timing depend on the specific case, so confirm the right path for your situation through consultation.

<h2 id="section-5">Permitted Activities and Restricted Occupations</h2>

### What You Can Do

- General employment in office, professional, and technical roles
- Business activity including business registration and incorporation
- Economic activity such as property registration and financial transactions
- Enrolling children in school and joining national health insurance

### Simple-Labor Restrictions

> **Practical Tip:** F-4 holders are restricted from simple-labor work. Cleaning, car washing, food delivery, and domestic help are among the occupations classified as restricted activities. The list is updated periodically, so check the announcements from the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr).

Starting work without first confirming your occupation falls within the permitted scope can lead to revocation of your status.

In particular, kitchen assistant work at restaurants and unskilled labor at construction sites often become problems even when you are the business owner, if you perform the work directly.

<h2 id="section-6">Common Sticking Points in F-4 Applications</h2>

### Gaps in Lineage Documentation

The most frequent issue is a break in the family register between the parent and grandparent generations.

Many records were lost during the period of division and migration, and supplementary affidavits or relative testimony statements are often needed to fill the gap.

### Mismatched Format on Home-Country Notarization

When kinship notarization issued in the home country doesn't conform to Korean format requirements, a request for correction is typically issued.

If this piece is weak, reviews commonly drag on for more than a month.

### Prior Immigration or Criminal Records

A history of working outside permitted occupations under H-2, overstaying, or criminal punishment for things like drunk driving or assault will trigger disciplinary screening.

In these cases, preparing in advance for the disciplinary screening matters far more than the application itself.

> **Caution:** With a record of drunk driving, assault, or similar offenses, there have been cases of status revocation or deportation even when basic eligibility was met. Outcomes depend on the timing of the ruling, the fine amount, and any repeat offenses, so a pre-review of your specific situation is essential.

### When Cases Are Referred to Disciplinary Screening

When the reviewer requests additional documents or refers the case to disciplinary screening, processing time stretches significantly.

At this stage, consistency between your personal statement and the supporting evidence is what determines the outcome.

<h2 id="section-7">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

### Q1. Once granted, is the F-4 visa valid for life?

You must apply to extend your stay every three years.

Renewal is generally routine absent disqualifying factors, but a new criminal record can lead to refusal.

### Q2. What happens if I work in a restricted simple-labor occupation on an F-4?

It is classified as activity outside your residency status and can result in status revocation or a fine.

When the occupation classification is unclear, confirm in advance with the Immigration Office.

### Q3. How do I prove that my parent held Korean nationality?

You need to obtain your parent's removed family register or family relations certificate.

From the home country, you'll need a household register and kinship notarization showing that your parent held Korean nationality at the time of birth.

The two sets of records must match the same individual.

### Q4. Can I bring my family to Korea on an F-4 visa?

Spouses and minor children can be sponsored on F-1 accompanying status.

Adult children must qualify for their own F-4 separately.

### Q5. Can I apply for an F-4 if I have a prior drunk driving conviction?

The outcome depends on the severity, when the ruling was issued, and whether it's a repeat offense.

Depending on the fine amount and timing, the case may be referred to disciplinary screening, so a pre-review is essential.

### Q6. How long does it take to get a decision after applying?

There is a standard processing timeline, but actual time varies with the jurisdiction, document supplementation, and whether the case goes to disciplinary screening.

For an accurate estimate, check with the office handling your case.
