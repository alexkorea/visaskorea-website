# How to Apply for Permission for Activities Outside Status of Stay: What Really Decides the Review

Permission for activities outside one's status of stay is a prior authorization required when a foreign national wants to add income-generating work or activities in a different field while still maintaining the activities allowed under their original status of stay.

This applies to almost all foreigners holding status categories with limited primary-activity scope — D-2, D-4, D-10, E-1 through E-7, and certain F-2 holders — when they want to teach at academies, do interpretation/translation, assist with research, or take on part-time work.

This guide covers timing, required documents, how to explain the relationship with your primary activity, and the difference from a change of workplace, all from a practical, in-the-field perspective.

<h2 id="section-1">When You Need Permission for Activities Outside Status of Stay</h2>

### When You Add Activities Beyond Your Primary Work

This permission is required "when you start work that goes beyond the activity scope of your current visa."

The most common cases are: a D-2 student working part-time at a cafe, an E-2 conversation instructor teaching at academies other than their main academy, or an E-7 worker taking on advisory work for an outside company.

This is where the trouble usually starts.

Even if you think it's just "a quick favor," Immigration often treats it as unauthorized employment from the moment you step outside your primary activity scope.

The first thing to check is: "Is this activity included in the activities permitted under my current status of stay?"

### How It Differs From Workplace Change or Addition Reports

A common point of confusion is the difference from a workplace change notification.

Workplace change/addition reports (for E categories) and permission for activities outside status of stay are separate systems.

Here's the core distinction.

If only the company changes within the same job category, it's a workplace change. If you start a new activity beyond the scope of your primary status, it falls under permission for activities outside status of stay.

In practice, getting this distinction wrong can lead to your application being rejected outright.

| Category | When Used | Typical Case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workplace change/addition report | Job change within the same status of stay scope | E-7 holder moving to a company in the same job category |
| Permission for activities outside status of stay | Adding activities outside the scope of the primary status | D-2 student taking part-time work |
| Status of stay change permission | When activity completely changes and a new visa is needed | Transitioning from D-10 to E-7 |

<h2 id="section-2">Who Can Apply and Where People Often Get Stuck</h2>

### Permitted Activity Scope by Status

Each status of stay has its own defined range of permissible outside activities.

- D-2 Student: Part-time work (with weekday/weekend hour limits and Korean language proficiency requirements)
- D-4 General Training: Limited part-time work after a certain period
- D-10 Job Seeker: Internships, short-term research assistance, and certain other activities
- E-1 through E-7 Professionals: Lectures, advisory work, research, and other professional-domain extensions beyond the primary job
- Certain F-2 holders: Additional activities when specific requirements are met

A point that's commonly overlooked: both "permitted hours" and "permitted fields" must be satisfied.

The most frequent mistake is assuming hours are enough, only to be blocked because the field doesn't qualify.

### What Actually Decides the Review

In real reviews, the first thing examiners look at is "whether the outside activity can coexist with the primary activity."

When a D-2 student applies for part-time work, low attendance or poor grades easily lead to denial on the grounds that the primary activity would suffer.

For E-category holders adding outside advisory work or lectures, weak consent from the primary employer, or hours overlapping with the primary job, can derail the application immediately.

If this explanation is weak, no amount of paperwork will save the result.

Since your specific situation depends on the individual case, it's safer to confirm the precise permitted scope through [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) notices combined with professional consultation.

<h2 id="section-3">The Application Process at a Glance</h2>

### Pre-Application Preparation

Applications generally follow this flow.

| Step | Content | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Step 1 | Confirm activity eligibility in advance | Check primary status of stay and remaining period |
| Step 2 | Obtain documents from inviting institution/employer | Business registration certificate, employment contract, etc. |
| Step 3 | Prepare your own documents | Passport, alien registration card, photo, etc. |
| Step 4 | Book appointment via HiKorea | Jurisdictional Immigration Office |
| Step 5 | Visit, submit, and undergo review | Additional documents may be requested |
| Step 6 | Receive result and permission sticker | Affixed to back of alien registration card or recorded in system |

The step where things usually get stuck is Step 2.

If the inviting institution's business registration status, social insurance enrollment, or wage levels don't meet the requirements, your personal documents being perfect won't move things forward.

### Timing and Processing Period

Permission for activities outside status of stay must be obtained before the activity begins.

If you start working first and apply later, retroactive approval is not granted, and you may be classified as engaging in unauthorized employment and fined.

Processing times vary by Immigration Office and may extend further if supplementary documents are requested.

For the fastest progression, we check the processing speed and likelihood of supplement requests by jurisdiction in advance.

> **Caution:** Starting an activity while the application is still pending can result in classification as illegal employment. You must wait for the result notification before beginning the activity.

### Common Required Documents

| Document | Description | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Application form | Integrated application form (Form No. 34) | HiKorea form |
| Passport & alien registration card | Original and copy | Copies should focus on the photo page |
| Standard-sized photo | Taken within last 6 months | White background |
| Primary status documents | Enrollment certificate, employment certificate, etc. | Varies by status |
| Activity-supporting documents | Employment contract, letter of appointment, lecture plan, etc. | Varies by activity field |
| Inviting institution documents | Business registration, corporate registration, etc. | Copies acceptable |

Official Ministry of Justice forms and guidance can be found at the [Ministry of Justice](https://www.moj.go.kr) and [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr).

### Documents People Often Miss

More important than the paperwork itself is "documentation showing the relationship with your primary activity."

- Consent letter from the primary employer or confirmation of activity compatibility
- Records showing your attendance/work status at the primary activity (grade reports, employment certificate)
- A schedule showing the outside activity hours don't overlap with the primary work

These documents aren't always on the mandatory list, but if an examiner suspects the activities aren't compatible, these are the first things they'll request.

This is especially decisive for D-2 students applying for part-time work and E-2 instructors adding outside lectures.

<h2 id="section-6">Common Reasons for Denial or Rejection</h2>

### When the Reviewer Concludes the Primary Activity Will Suffer

This is the most frequently seen reason for denial.

For D-2 students, the combination of poor grades and low attendance often results in outright denial of part-time work.

For E-category professionals, denial happens when primary work hours overlap with the outside activity, or when consent from the primary employer is unclear — reviewers conclude the primary work would be destabilized.

Conversely, the more the outside activity connects to your primary field, the higher the approval likelihood.

### When the Activity Falls Outside Your Status Scope

Applying for an activity in a field that your status doesn't permit results in immediate rejection.

The most common example is D-2 students applying for part-time work in restricted industries such as nightlife venues or gambling-related businesses.

An E-1 professor applying for a general clerical position unrelated to their primary work is blocked for the same reason.

### Inconsistencies and Factual Mismatches

If the activity hours, location, and pay stated on the application don't match what's in the employment contract or consent letter, reviewers conclude the factual basis is unstable, and the application is blocked.

In practice, this issue may seem minor but is one of the most frequent stumbling blocks.

A single inconsistent line can change the outcome, so aligning all documents at once before applying is essential.

> **Practical Tip:** Before applying, make sure the "activity start date," "weekly hours," and "monthly compensation" are consistent across every document. Inconsistencies between documents account for over 80% of supplement requests.

<h2 id="section-7">Post-Approval Management and Cautions</h2>

### When You Step Outside the Approved Scope

Permission is only valid within the scope of the activity you applied for.

Even at the same company, a change in duties — or an additional workplace within the same role — requires a new application.

If working hours exceed the approved limits, both you and your employer can be fined.

### Departure, Re-Entry, and Outside Activity Permission

The validity period of the permission is linked to the remaining period of your primary status of stay.

If you change or newly obtain a status of stay, the outside activity permission terminates automatically.

After re-entry, if your primary status of stay is maintained, the existing permission remains valid for its remaining period — but if the activity content changes, you must reapply.

Since detailed operational guidelines change frequently, it's safer to confirm how the rules apply to your specific situation via the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr) along with professional guidance.

<h2 id="section-8">FAQ</h2>

### Q1. What happens if I work briefly without obtaining outside activity permission?

It's classified as unauthorized employment, which can result in fines, disadvantages when extending or changing your status, and even grounds for deportation.

Even one or two months of short-term part-time work for a D-2 student is treated the same way when discovered after the fact, so it's safer to obtain permission before starting.

### Q2. What requirements must a D-2 student meet to take part-time work?

Korean language proficiency (TOPIK level), academic stage (undergraduate vs. graduate), working hours/days, and industry restrictions all apply.

The standards are adjusted annually under operational guidelines, so check [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) notices to see which bracket you fall into.

### Q3. Does an E-2 instructor need outside activity permission to teach at a different academy?

Even within the same conversation-teaching field, teaching at academies other than your contracted one is often a separate permission matter.

To avoid getting stuck, you need to check that it doesn't infringe on your primary academy's hours and that the outside academy meets the qualifying requirements.

### Q4. Do F-2 visa holders need outside activity permission?

For F-2, the activity scope differs by sub-category.

In principle, broad employment is allowed, but certain sub-categories and certain industries do require outside activity permission, so you should first confirm your specific F-2 sub-category.

### Q5. Can I start the activity after applying but before receiving the result?

No.

Permission takes effect based on the "decision notification," not the "application."

Starting the activity during review can be treated as unauthorized employment.

### Q6. Is one approval enough, or do I need to extend it?

Permission ties together the activity type, period, and location — if any of those conditions change, you must apply again.

When you extend your primary status of stay, the outside activity often requires a separate extension or fresh application as well.
