# Overseas Korean Visa (F-4): Eligibility Requirements and Application Process

The F-4 overseas Korean visa is a residence status granted to former Korean nationals or their lineal descendants who have acquired foreign citizenship.

Eligible applicants are foreign-national overseas Koreans whose own nationality, or that of a parent or grandparent, was once Korean. Simple labor activities are restricted under this status.

This guide covers F-4 eligibility criteria, key points for passing the criminal background review, document preparation, the application procedure, and the bottlenecks that trip people up most often.

<h2 id="section-1">What Is the F-4 Visa and Who Can Receive It?</h2>

### Legal Basis and Definition

The F-4 is a residence status granted to foreign-national overseas Koreans under the Act on the Immigration and Legal Status of Overseas Koreans (the Overseas Koreans Act).

The exact statutory text can be reviewed at the [Korean Law Information Center](https://www.law.go.kr).

The core idea is this.

The applicant must be an overseas Korean — or a lineal descendant of one — who emigrated abroad around the time of the founding of the Republic of Korea and subsequently acquired foreign citizenship.

### Categories of Eligible Applicants

Eligible applicants fall into two broad branches.

The first thing to determine is whether the applicant personally held Korean nationality in the past.

If the applicant was once a Korean national, a loss-of-nationality report must be filed in advance.

If one of the parents or grandparents was a Korean national, the direct bloodline must be proven through the family registry, the old hojeok (jejeok), or a basic certificate.

For overseas Koreans from China or the former Soviet Union in particular, a separate procedure applies.

Because the governing provisions vary depending on the applicant's individual situation, it is safer to confirm eligibility through a consultation.

### Activities Not Permitted Under F-4

The F-4 allows a wide range of activities, but not everything is permitted.

| Activity Type | Permitted? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| General office or professional employment | Yes | Reporting obligation applies |
| Business or investment activities | Yes | Separate business registration required |
| Simple labor activities | Restricted | Construction sites, restaurant serving, etc. |
| Sectors violating public order or morals | Prohibited | Some gambling and adult entertainment industries |

The scope of "simple labor" is defined by Ministry of Justice notices and changes frequently, so it is safer to check announcements on [Hi Korea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) as well.

<h2 id="section-2">F-4 Eligibility Documents — Where Applicants Get Stuck Most Often in Practice</h2>

### Bloodline Documentation

This is usually where applications stall.

No matter how thick the file is, if the bloodline link breaks at a single point, a supplementation request follows immediately.

The basic documents are as follows.

- The applicant's basic certificate or birth certificate
- The parents' or grandparents' jejeok (former family registry) or family relation certificate
- A document showing the loss of Korean nationality
- Proof of foreign citizenship acquisition

If the Chinese-character spellings, family origin (bon-gwan), or place of birth on an old jejeok do not match the foreign birth certificate, proving same-person identity becomes a roadblock.

When this link is weak, supporting evidence such as relatives' statements, photographs, or remittance records must be submitted as well.

### Documents for the Criminal Background Review

At the F-4 issuance stage, the criminal background (sabeom) review is strict.

This is the area applicants most commonly overlook.

- A criminal record certificate issued by the country of residence (with apostille or consular authentication)
- Consent form for a domestic Korean criminal record check
- Records of any dispositions related to DUI, sexual offenses, or property crimes

Even a single DUI disposition can be grounds for denial, and if the fine exceeds a certain threshold, issuance can be put on hold.

Because the thresholds are adjusted each year through operational guidelines, the only way to know how they apply is to verify against your own disposition record.

### Translation and Notarization of Documents

For documents issued abroad, Korean translation along with an apostille or consular authentication is essentially mandatory.

> **Caution:** If you attach only the translation and skip consular authentication, your documents will be rejected and you'll have to start from scratch.

Whether translation notarization or an apostille is needed depends on whether the issuing country is a Hague Convention member.

<h2 id="section-3">F-4 Application Procedure, Step by Step</h2>

### Applying at an Overseas Mission vs. Changing Status in Korea

There are two pathways to obtaining an F-4.

| Pathway | Where to Apply | Eligible Applicants |
|---|---|---|
| Visa issuance | Korean overseas mission | Residents abroad |
| Status change | Local immigration office | Legal residents already in Korea |
| Certificate of visa issuance | Domestic immigration office, then sent to the applicant | When the reason for entry is clear |

Under the overseas-mission pathway, you receive the visa after document review, enter Korea, and complete foreign resident registration within 90 days of entry.

The domestic status change is the process of switching from your current status to F-4, and your prior residence history in that status is factored into the review.

### Processing Time and Order of Steps

The procedure flows in this order.

1. Preliminary eligibility review (bloodline and criminal record)
2. Document collection in both home country and Korea
3. Translation, apostille, and notarization
4. Application form preparation and submission
5. Response to supplementation requests
6. Result notification and certificate issuance

Processing times vary by mission and by immigration office, and a single supplementation request can add one to two months.

In practice, it pays to plan your filing strategy around the jurisdiction that can move the fastest.

<h2 id="section-4">F-4 Criminal Background Review — When You Have a DUI or Prior Record</h2>

### Common Grounds for Denial

In actual reviews, dispositions the applicant considered trivial are often the ones that derail the case.

Typical grounds for criminal-background denial include the following.

- DUI dispositions (including fines, license suspension, or revocation)
- Prior convictions for assault or injury
- Arranging prostitution, or simple participation
- Drug-related dispositions
- Involvement in voice phishing or fraud
- Violations of the Immigration Control Act (illegal stay, false documents)

If any one of these applies, F-4 issuance may be deferred or denied.

### How to Respond After a Denial

A denial notice is not the end of the road.

The response path depends on the situation.

- Reapply after the required time since the disposition has elapsed
- Administrative appeal or administrative litigation
- Reapply after amnesty or restoration of rights
- Enter under another status and change to F-4 later

Which path is viable depends on the type of disposition, the date the sentence became final, and how much time has passed.

This is an area where the determination hinges on actually reviewing the disposition record, so a prior consultation is the safer route.

### Special Cases for Overseas Koreans from China and the Former Soviet Union

For overseas Koreans from China or the former Soviet Union, separate operational guidelines apply in some respects.

Whether F-4 is granted depends on the applicant's region of birth, whether parents or grandparents were entered in the Korean family registry, and any history of nationality recovery.

It is also common to try to convert from H-2 (Work and Visit) to F-4, but if the supporting documentation for eligibility is thin, the case will stall at the H-2 stage.

For detailed operational standards, you should also consult the guidance from the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr).

<h2 id="section-5">F-4 Residence Management — What Happens After You Get It Matters More</h2>

### Foreign Resident Registration and Period of Stay

Once you receive the F-4, you must complete foreign resident registration within 90 days of entry.

The period of stay is typically granted in units of one to three years, and an extension must be filed before expiration.

Missing the extension window can put you in a situation where you must leave the country and reapply.

### Reporting Employment Activities

The F-4 allows employment, but not every job is automatically permitted.

The question of whether work qualifies as "simple labor" is a common sticking point.

> **Practical tip:** For jobs with ambiguous duties (e.g., general office work combined with on-site assistance), it is safer to confirm eligibility before accepting the position.

Employment-activity reporting can be done at the local immigration office or through [Hi Korea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr).

### Transitioning to F-5 (Permanent Residency)

An F-4 holder who meets certain requirements can convert to F-5 permanent resident status.

Length of stay, income, and criminal record are all considered together, and the thresholds are adjusted each year through operational guidelines.

The most accurate way to know whether you meet this year's standards is to bring your documents in and have them checked directly.

<h2 id="section-6">Five Practical Sticking Points That Trip Applicants Up</h2>

### Broken Bloodline Documentation

When a grandparent's jejeok was lost around the Korean War era, the chain must be reconnected with relatives' statements and supplementary evidence.

This kind of work is safer to handle alongside an administrative attorney experienced in reading hojeok and jejeok records.

### Inconsistent Names and Places of Birth

It is common for Chinese-character names, romanized names, and places of birth to appear differently across Korean and foreign documents.

A separate set of same-person identity documents has to be prepared.

### Handling Past Dual Nationality Periods

If you had a period of dual nationality in the past, the timing of your loss-of-nationality report affects the review.

### Failed H-2 to F-4 Conversions

There are many cases where applicants attempt to convert from H-2 to F-4 but are denied because their eligibility documentation is insufficient.

A pre-submission document review is the safer course before attempting conversion.

### Omitted Criminal-Record Information

When a disposition the applicant had forgotten surfaces through the criminal-record check, disclosing it up front and addressing it tends to produce better results than scrambling to fix things after the fact.

<h2 id="section-7">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

**Q1. If my parents never held Korean nationality, can I still get an F-4?**

If even one of your grandparents was once a Korean national, you can apply.

Proof of the direct bloodline is the key.

**Q2. I have one DUI fine on my record. Can I still apply?**

The outcome depends on when the disposition occurred, the amount of the fine, and how much time has passed since.

It is safer to have your disposition record reviewed in advance.

**Q3. I'm an ethnic Korean with Chinese nationality. Which route should I take?**

You can either enter on an H-2 Work and Visit visa and convert to F-4 after proving eligibility, or apply for an F-4 visa directly from your home country.

The recommended route depends on the strength of your family relationship documentation.

**Q4. What happens if I'm caught doing simple labor on an F-4?**

You can have your residence status cancelled or be ordered to leave the country for an activity-scope violation.

This is not a matter that ends with a fine, so confirming the job category in advance is the safer path.

**Q5. How long does it take from application to F-4 issuance?**

If all documents are in order the first time, it can be processed within a few weeks; if supplementation requests come in, it can stretch to one to three months.

Processing times vary by jurisdiction, so filing where it moves fastest is to your advantage.

**Q6. The criminal record certificate I got abroad isn't in Korean. What do I do?**

You must attach a notarized translation along with an apostille (or consular authentication).

The procedure depends on whether the issuing country is a Hague Convention member.
