# Foreign Resident Registration 30-Day Rule and Fines: Where Practitioners Get Stuck Most Often

Foreign nationals planning to stay in Korea for more than 90 days must complete their foreign resident registration at the competent immigration office **within 90 days** of their entry date. After registration, changes such as place of residence, passport details, or workplace must generally be reported **within 14 or 15 days** to avoid fines.

This applies to D, E, and F category long-term residents, international students, marriage immigrants, investors, accompanying family members — essentially everyone in long-term status, excluding short-term visitors (C category).

This article unpacks the registration, change-reporting, and re-registration deadlines that are commonly grouped together under the "30-day rule" in search queries, along with the fine flow when deadlines are missed — all from a practitioner's standpoint.

<h2 id="section-1">What the 90-Day Foreign Registration Rule Actually Means</h2>

### When does the 90-day clock start?

The reference point is the **date the entry stamp was issued**.

It is not the visa issuance date or the ticket purchase date.

This is precisely where most people get tripped up in practice.

You must complete registration by the 90th day counted from the entry stamp date in your passport.

If the 90th day falls on a holiday, the next business day still counts — but it's safer to act well before that point.

### Where does the term "30-day rule" come from?

Many people type "foreign registration 30 days" into search engines.

This phrasing emerges when **the deadline for reporting post-registration changes** gets blended with the 90-day registration deadline in people's minds.

Under the law, reporting deadlines branch into 14, 15, or 30 days depending on the matter.

In particular, the rule for change of residence is **within 15 days** of moving in.

Workplace change/addition reports and passport reissuance reports have their own separate deadlines, so memorizing everything as "30 days" will get you stuck in practice.

<h2 id="section-2">The Registration Process Within 90 Days</h2>

### Where do you apply?

It is handled at your competent immigration office or branch office.

You must go to the office that has jurisdiction over your place of residence.

Visit appointments are typically booked in advance through [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr).

Many offices will not process same-day visits without a reservation.

### Standard documents required

| Item | Details | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Application form | One integrated application form | Available at the office or via HiKorea |
| Passport | Original + copy | Personal information page |
| Photo | Standard-size color photo | Taken within the last 6 months |
| Proof of residence | Lease agreement, dormitory confirmation, etc. | Verifying actual residence is essential |
| Status-specific documents | Enrollment certificate, employment certificate, business registration, etc. | Varies by visa status |
| Fee | Government-published fee + administrative processing fee | Card / bank transfer accepted |

More important than the documents themselves is **consistency in your proof of residence**.

If the address on the lease, your actual residence, and the address on the application don't match, this is usually where things stall.

### Processing time

Times vary significantly between offices.

It typically takes 2 to 4 weeks from application to issuance of the alien registration card, but seasonal load and office-specific delays often push this longer.

<h2 id="section-3">Deadlines for Reporting Changes in Status or Residence</h2>

### Reporting a change of residence (move-in report)

The rule is **within 15 days** of moving.

Previously you had to visit the immigration office, but now a move-in report at the competent district office or community center substitutes for the foreigner change-of-residence report.

You must bring your alien registration card when filing the move-in report.

### Reporting a workplace change or addition

For employment-based categories such as E-1 through E-7, a workplace change or addition requires either prior permission or a notification procedure.

Each status splits into **prior permission required** or **post-event notification required** — this is where confusion happens most.

Even for post-event notifications, 15 days is generally the rule.

### Passport changes, nationality changes, status changes

If you have been reissued a new passport, you must report the new passport details.

Separate deadlines apply if your nationality has changed or if there are personal status events such as birth or death.

In practice, the most common slip-up is forgetting to report a passport change after reissuance.

| Reporting item | Deadline | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Initial foreign registration | Within 90 days of entry | Competent immigration office |
| Change of residence | Within 15 days of moving in | District office / community center |
| Workplace change or addition (post-notification) | Within 15 days of the change | Competent immigration office |
| Personal information change such as passport reissuance | Within 15 days of the change | Competent immigration office |
| Status of stay change permission | Before the cause arises | Competent immigration office |

Deadlines are based on the [Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr) and its enforcement decree, but some provisions are updated periodically, so the version that applies to your specific case should be confirmed with the competent authority.

<h2 id="section-4">How Fines Are Actually Imposed</h2>

### Fines are not a flat amount

A commonly missed point: fines are **not a single fixed amount, but are graduated based on the duration of the violation, the number of occurrences, and the reason**.

How many days late, first-time vs. repeat, self-reporting vs. discovered through enforcement — all of these shift the amount.

Because the cost varies case by case, we provide accurate figures during a free consultation.

### The aftermath is heavier than the fine itself

What's more worrying than the fine itself is **the disadvantage in subsequent extension and status-change reviews**.

A record of failing to meet reporting obligations can weaken your reliability score in future extension reviews.

For permanent residency (F-5) or naturalization applications, this record can be the deciding factor.

In actual reviews, examiners look less at "did you pay the fine" and more at "did you faithfully fulfill your reporting obligations."

### Self-reporting vs. enforcement detection

Even if you've missed the deadline, the outcome differs between voluntarily visiting the office to self-report and having the violation surface during another application process.

That's where the gap appears.

If you've already missed a deadline, the order of your next actions determines the result.

<h2 id="section-5">Exemptions and Exception Cases</h2>

### Cases excluded from registration

Short-term visitors of 90 days or less (C category, visa-free entrants) have no registration obligation.

Holders of diplomatic (A-1), official (A-2), and agreement (A-3) statuses, along with certain treaty-based exemption categories, are subject to different rules.

### The trap when departure is imminent

"I'm leaving soon, so I don't really need to register, right?" is a common question.

It looks simple on the surface, but it's actually risky.

If you leave before reaching 90 days from entry, the registration obligation is waived. But if departure is delayed by even a single day past that point, the violation begins from that moment.

If your travel or flight schedule has variables, **operating against an 89-day mark** is the safer approach.

### Whether registration carries over upon re-entry

If you have completed foreign registration and then depart and re-enter Korea, whether your registration remains active or is cancelled depends on the length of your absence and your re-entry permission status.

This is an area where applying the [Immigration Office](https://www.immigration.go.kr) guidance directly often leaves people uncertain about which side their own case falls on.

<h2 id="section-6">Reissuance of a Lost or Damaged Alien Registration Card</h2>

### Loss reporting and reissuance deadline

If you lose your alien registration card, you must apply for reissuance **within 14 days** of becoming aware of the loss.

The same procedure applies when the card is damaged to the point of being unreadable.

### Common sticking points during reissuance

Drafting the loss circumstance report is where most people get stuck.

You have to record when, where, and how it was lost in chronological order — and if your memory is hazy, that's where the drafting falls apart.

The first thing to do is to clearly nail down the time of loss and the last time you used it.

| Category | Loss report | Damage reissuance | Renewal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Application deadline | Within 14 days of awareness | When the cause arises | Before expiration |
| Key documents | Loss circumstance report + photo | Damaged card + photo | Existing card + photo |
| Fee | Government-published fee + administrative processing fee | Government-published fee + administrative processing fee | Government-published fee + administrative processing fee |

<h2 id="section-7">Violation Patterns That Actually Come Up Often</h2>

### Pattern 1: Schedules pile up right after entry, and 90 days slips by

This is common among international students and new hires.

Right after entry, school enrollment, finding a place to live, and visits to the community center all stack up, and registration gets pushed back until the 90 days are gone.

In this case, the fine comes paired with a disadvantage in future extension reviews.

### Pattern 2: Missing the move-in report after relocating

This is missing the 15-day deadline for change of residence.

It's especially frequent among people who shuffle between short-term rentals, boarding houses, or dormitories.

A safer rule of thumb is to act by the weekend following your move.

### Pattern 3: Missing the personal info change report after passport reissuance

This happens when someone gets a new passport in their home country, re-enters Korea on the new passport, and forgets to report the new passport details to the immigration office.

A gap here means the documents won't go through cleanly in the next extension application.

### Pattern 4: Registration cancelled between departure and re-entry

After a long absence and re-entry, your foreign registration may already be in a cancelled state.

People often believe their registration is still active, only to discover it has been cancelled when checking at the bank, mobile carrier, or hospital.

<h2 id="section-8">FAQ</h2>

### Q1. If I leave Korea before 90 days are up, do I have to complete foreign registration?

If you **depart before reaching 90 days**, the registration obligation does not arise.

However, if there's any chance your departure date might shift, it's safer to use 89 days as your line in the sand.

### Q2. I'm a few days past the 90-day registration deadline — what should I do?

Approaching the competent immigration office through self-reporting first generally produces a better outcome than being caught through enforcement.

That said, the exact handling depends on how many days late you are and what status you hold, so checking your specific case takes priority.

### Q3. I moved — if I file the move-in report at the community center, do I still need to visit the immigration office separately?

In principle, the move-in report at the community center substitutes for the foreigner change-of-residence report.

But there are on-the-spot checks — carrying your alien registration card, ensuring the lease agreement matches, and so on — so a pre-visit check is the safer move.

### Q4. I lost my alien registration card and I already have an overseas business trip scheduled. What should I do?

The rule is to apply for reissuance within 14 days of becoming aware of the loss, and if your departure schedule overlaps with the reissuance processing window, you need to check with the office about whether interim measures are available.

In this kind of case, the scheduling conflict itself has to be untangled first, so booking a consultation to set priorities is the fastest route.

### Q5. My workplace changed — does the post-event notification deadline apply, or does this require prior permission?

For each of the E-1 through E-7 categories, the split between prior permission required and post-event notification differs.

The applicable criteria for each status are partially adjusted each year, so for this year's rules on your status, please confirm via consultation.

### Q6. I've paid the fine — will this affect a future permanent residency (F-5) application?

Paying the fine itself does not strip you of eligibility, but a history of failing to meet reporting obligations can factor into the reliability assessment during permanent residency or naturalization reviews.

There have been recent cases where similar histories produced split outcomes, so the actual impact on your record requires individual review.
