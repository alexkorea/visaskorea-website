# Naturalization Interview Past Topics: Real Questions and How to Prepare Answers

The naturalization interview is decided less by Korean language ability and more by **how naturally you can explain your personal background and life in Korea**.

Applicants for general, simplified, and special naturalization all go through the same interview. Those who have completed Level 5 of the Korea Immigration and Integration Program (KIIP) are exempt, but everyone else must take it.

This article covers the past topics that come up most often in actual interviews, the scoring points, and the parts that people most commonly overlook when preparing their answers.

<h2 id="section-1">How the Naturalization Interview Is Structured</h2>

### The Interview Is Not a Korean Language Test

The naturalization interview is not simply a Korean conversation test.

The examiner is checking **whether the applicant has the basic qualifications to be a member of Korean society**, and whether you personally know the contents of the documents you submitted.

Even if your paperwork looks perfect, if you can't state your own address properly during the interview or you get confused about family relationships, suspicion follows immediately.

In practice, many people with fluent Korean still get tripped up by their answers, while others with awkward pronunciation pass because they answer clearly and directly.

### Who Is Exempt and Who Must Take the Interview

You can be exempt from the interview if you pass the Level 5 comprehensive evaluation of the Korea Immigration and Integration Program (KIIP), or if you fall under certain grounds set out in the Enforcement Rule of the Nationality Act.

All other applicants must take the interview.

The exact exemption grounds should be checked through the [Ministry of Justice Enforcement Rule of the Nationality Act](https://www.law.go.kr) and the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr). Operating policies have changed recently, so confirm whether you qualify through a consultation.

<h2 id="section-2">The Most Common Past Topics — Personal Background</h2>

### Name, Date of Birth, Address, Family Relations

The first questions in the interview are almost always the same set.

- Your name and its Chinese character form
- Date of birth
- Current home address (down to the road name address)
- Names and dates of birth of parents, spouse, and children
- Name and location of your workplace or school

It looks simple on the surface, but **a surprising number of applicants cannot state their own address correctly in Korean.**

In particular, if you've only memorized the lot-number address even though the road-name address changed long ago, you'll freeze the moment the examiner asks again.

You also need to be able to state your spouse's and children's dates of birth without confusing the solar and lunar calendars.

### Entry History and Visa Record

You'll be asked when you first came to Korea, what visa you entered on, and what visas you changed to afterward.

If you're shaky here, it leaves the impression that "this person doesn't even know their own residency history."

If the entry date on your application doesn't match what you say out loud, things unravel fast.

> **Practical tip:** Before the interview, the fastest way to prepare is to summarize your alien registration card, the entry stamps in your passport, and your visa change history on a single sheet and memorize it.

<h2 id="section-3">Past Topics on Life in Korea and General Knowledge</h2>

### Basic Facts About Korea

Common questions include:

- The capital of Korea
- The name of the current president
- The name and meaning of the national flag
- The first verse of the national anthem
- The four major Korean holidays
- The meaning of major national holidays such as Liberation Day and National Foundation Day

Of these, **the first verse of the national anthem comes up almost every time.**

If you don't know the lyrics, you may even be asked to sing it on the spot.

### Questions About Daily Life

You may be asked the name of the nearest subway station, market, hospital, or community service center to your home.

If you stumble here, it leads to doubt about "whether you actually live in Korea."

In particular, when there is suspicion of fake residency, the examiner digs deeper with specific questions about neighborhood geography, supermarket names, and bus routes.

### Korean History and Historical Figures

You'll be asked about the achievements of representative figures such as King Sejong, Yi Sun-sin, An Jung-geun, and Kim Gu.

Major historical events also come up often — the creation of Hangul, the Imjin War, the March 1st Movement, and the Korean War.

You don't need to explain at length. **One clear sentence covering the essentials is enough.**

<h2 id="section-4">Reasons for Naturalization and Plans for Life in Korea</h2>

### Why Do You Want to Become a Korean Citizen?

This is one of the trickier questions.

Answering only "because I like Korea" is weak.

What the examiner wants to see is **that your life is actually rooted in Korea and that you plan to keep living here.**

Concrete grounds — family, work, your children's education, property, social ties — need to appear in your answer.

Speaking briefly and clearly about your own situation scores higher than rambling on in the abstract.

### Your Job and Income in Korea

You'll be asked about your occupation, the name of your company, your title, how long you've worked there, and your monthly income.

If what you say here doesn't match what you wrote on the application, it becomes a problem immediately.

If you're self-employed, you need to be able to answer about the location of your business, your main clients, and the type of business you run.

<h2 id="section-5">Special Questions in F-6 Marriage Migrant Simplified Naturalization Interviews</h2>

### Questions About Your Spouse and Married Life

Simplified naturalization applicants through marriage face a more intense interview than general naturalization applicants.

Common questions include:

- How you first met your spouse
- The date and location of your wedding
- Where you went on your honeymoon
- The names and dates of birth of your spouse's parents
- Your spouse's occupation and workplace
- Your everyday routine together (meals, going out, holidays, etc.)

If you and your spouse give conflicting answers here, it leads directly to suspicion of a sham marriage.

### Additional Questions If You Have Children

You may be asked the name of your child's school, the name of their homeroom teacher, their grade, and their usual route to and from school.

These questions check whether you are actually involved in raising the child.

If you're weak here, the genuineness of the family relationship comes under doubt.

<h2 id="section-6">Scoring Criteria and Passing Requirements</h2>

| Evaluation Item | Main Check Points | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Korean language ability | Comprehension of questions, natural answers | Communication matters more than pronunciation |
| Personal background | Accuracy on address, family, entry history | Must match the paperwork |
| General knowledge | Basic national facts, history, historical figures | Focus on essentials |
| Adjustment to life in Korea | Residence, workplace, neighbors | Verifies actual residency |
| Reason for naturalization | Intent and grounds for settling in Korea | Specificity matters |

Scoring is often decided not by adding up each item but by **the overall impression and whether any red flags came up.**

If you stumble badly on one item, the overall impression wobbles.

<h2 id="section-7">Comparing Interview Intensity by Naturalization Type</h2>

| Naturalization Type | Interview Intensity | Main Check Points |
|---|---|---|
| General naturalization | Moderate | Korean language, general knowledge, residency history |
| Simplified naturalization (F-6) | Strong | Genuineness of marriage, spousal consistency |
| Simplified naturalization (F-2/F-5) | Moderate to strong | Actual residency, livelihood base |
| Special naturalization | Low to moderate | Mostly identity verification |

> **Note:** Even for special naturalization, the interview is not always fully exempt — it varies by case.
> Interview intensity for your type should be confirmed with the immigration office that has jurisdiction over your area.

<h2 id="section-8">Actual Cases of People Failing the Interview</h2>

### Perfect Paperwork, Stuck in the Interview

This happens when an administrative scrivener prepares the paperwork but the applicant hasn't really internalized what's in it.

When the examiner points at a single line on the application and asks, "You wrote this yourself, right? Explain this part," and you can't answer, the credibility of the entire file is shaken.

No matter how thick the file is, you won't pass if you can't explain its contents yourself.

### Marriage Migrant Cases Where Spouses Give Conflicting Answers

When couples walk into the interview without coordinating in advance, their answers diverge on small things like the wedding anniversary, how they address relatives, or what they usually eat.

In a recent similar case, the couple were asked separately and gave different dates, which ended in a re-interview.

We'll guide you on detailed preparation based on your specific situation during the consultation.

### Older Applicants With Limited Korean

In special naturalization or simplified naturalization cases for ethnic Koreans, older applicants with limited Korean often get stuck here.

In these cases, whether interpretation can be requested and how to prepare for the interview can differ, so check with the responsible authority in advance.

<h2 id="section-9">Pre-Interview Preparation Checklist</h2>

- Memorize your alien registration card and passport details
- Confirm both your road-name address and your lot-number address
- Organize the names, dates of birth, and occupations of all family members
- Organize your entry history and visa change history
- Practice the lyrics and melody of the first verse of the national anthem
- Review the president, the capital, and the meanings of national holidays
- Confirm the names of the nearest subway station, market, and community service center
- (Marriage migrants) Check that your answers match your spouse's

> **Practical tip:** The fastest way to prepare is to draft a list of expected questions and have family or friends run mock interviews with you 2 to 3 times.

<h2 id="section-10">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

**Q1. Will I automatically fail the naturalization interview if my Korean is poor?**

Even with limited Korean, you can still pass if you can answer questions about your personal background and the basics.

That said, if you can't communicate at all, it becomes difficult, and completing the Immigration and Integration Program is effectively necessary.

**Q2. If I complete Level 5 of the Immigration and Integration Program (KIIP), am I fully exempt from the interview?**

Those who pass the KIIP Level 5 comprehensive evaluation are exempt from the interview.

However, exemption criteria can shift depending on operating policy, so confirm whether it applies to you with the responsible authority.

**Q3. If I fail the interview, when can I retake it?**

Usually a re-interview is offered after a certain waiting period, but the number of re-interviews and the interval between them vary by case.

If you don't pass the re-interview either, the application itself can be denied, so passing the first interview is the safest path.

**Q4. Do examiners ever ask questions in languages other than Korean?**

In principle the interview is conducted in Korean, and interpretation support is limited.

It's safest to answer in Korean yourself.

**Q5. For marriage migrant interviews, does the spouse come in too?**

Sometimes the spouse sits in, and sometimes they are questioned separately.

Couples may be split up and questioned separately, so coordinating in advance is necessary.

**Q6. When will I be notified of my naturalization interview date?**

After you file the application, the immigration office notifies each applicant individually after a certain processing period.

The window between notification and the interview date can be short, so it's safer to start preparing from the application stage.
