# E-7 Visa Education Requirements and Career Standards by Occupation: A Complete Guide

The E-7 visa is built around a basic framework of a bachelor's degree or higher, a matching major, and at least one year of relevant experience — but the requirements shift depending on the occupational code.
It targets foreign professionals, and applicants must satisfy the requirements tied to one of the 86 designated occupational codes.
This guide walks through the education and career standards by occupation, alternatives when your major doesn't match, and the specific points where applications actually get split during review.

<h2 id="section-1">The Basic Framework of E-7 Visa Education Requirements</h2>

The E-7 looks like a single visa, but it's actually divided into 86 occupational codes.
The educational baseline differs by occupation, and whether career experience can substitute for a degree also varies.
The first thing to check is which category your specific occupation falls under.

### The Standard Line: Bachelor's Degree + 1 Year of Experience

The most common baseline requires meeting one of the following two structures.

- A bachelor's degree in the relevant field + at least 1 year of experience in that field
- A master's degree or higher in the relevant field (no experience required)

The bachelor's degree must, in principle, come from an accredited institution in your home country, and your major must match the occupation.
Even when your major looks similar on the surface, mismatches with the code's expected discipline typically trip applicants up at this stage.

### When Career Experience Can Substitute for Education

Even without a bachelor's degree, some occupations allow entry with 5 or more years of relevant experience.
That said, whether this path is recognized varies by occupation, and the burden of proof for career documentation is significantly heavier than on the degree track.
In actual review, employment certificates must clearly spell out job duties, duration, and position to have any chance of approval.

> **Note:** When substituting career experience for education, applicants are often asked to provide additional proof of the home-country employer's existence — business registration certificates, social insurance enrollment records, and the like. If proof of the company's existence is weak, the career experience itself may be disqualified.

<h2 id="section-2">Education and Career Standards for the 67 E-7-1 Professional Occupations</h2>

E-7-1 groups together managerial and professional-level occupations.
Wage requirements based on Gross National Income (GNI) also apply, so education alone isn't the end of the story.

### The Managerial Line (Executives, Department Heads)

This line typically requires a master's degree or higher + 3 or more years of relevant experience, or a bachelor's degree + 5 or more years of experience.
Representative codes include corporate planning manager, human resources/labor manager, and financial and insurance manager.
The fact that the position is genuinely at the managerial level must be clearly reflected in the organizational chart and job description.

### The Professional Line (Engineers, Researchers, Designers)

This line accepts a bachelor's degree + 1 year of experience, or a master's degree or higher on its own.
That said, IT and STEM occupations face the strictest scrutiny on whether your major actually matches.
Even if you majored in computer science, if the major name printed on your degree certificate doesn't align with the occupational code, requests for supplementary documentation will follow.

| Occupation Category | Education Standard | Career Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Managerial | Master's or bachelor's | Master's 3+ yrs / Bachelor's 5+ yrs | Proof of executive/department head role required |
| Professional | Bachelor's or higher | Bachelor's 1+ yr / Master's none | Strict major-matching review |
| Some Skilled Workers | Bachelor's or 5+ yrs experience | Career substitution possible | Recognition varies by occupation |
| Office Workers | Bachelor's or higher | Bachelor's 1+ yr | Demonstrating job-specific expertise is key |

When the major doesn't match, a common practical approach is to bundle minor coursework, double majors, relevant certifications, and related work history into a supplementary explanation letter.
However, the room for such supplementation varies by occupational code, so reviewing how far your specific case can stretch beforehand is essential.

<h2 id="section-3">The E-7-2 Semi-Professional and E-7-4 Skilled Worker Lines</h2>

For E-7-2 and E-7-4, career experience and wages weigh more heavily in review than education.
The key point is that while bachelor's degree requirements are loosened or waived, other requirements become stricter in return.

### E-7-2 Semi-Professional Education Requirements

Duty-free shop salespeople, hotel and condominium reception clerks, and air transport office workers fall into this category.
Requirements are generally an associate degree or higher + 1 year of experience, or a bachelor's degree or higher on its own.
Since many of these are service-industry roles, Korean language proficiency (such as TOPIK scores) functions as bonus points.

### The E-7-4 Skilled Worker Line

The main path involves converting from an E-9, E-10, or H-2 visa after working in Korea for 4 or more years.
Rather than education, evaluation centers on length of domestic employment, wage level, and Korean language scores.
Because it operates on a points system, you need to calculate your own score sheet to see when application makes sense.

> **Practical Tip:** The E-7-4 has an annual quota, so the cutoff score fluctuates each year. If your score is tight, the fastest move is to strengthen bonus-point categories first — certifications, Korean language scores, continuous employment. The exact cutoff applied in any given year shifts with each announcement, so verification before applying is necessary.

<h2 id="section-4">Where Applications Split on Major-Matching Review</h2>

Whether your major matches the code is the single biggest variable.
Even with stacks of supporting documents, a weak explanation of major-to-occupation matching can derail everything.

### IT and STEM Occupations

For roles like computer systems designer/analyst, database developer, and systems/network administrator, the orthodox majors are computer science, information and communications, or software engineering.
Math, physics, and statistics majors are sometimes recognized, but the connection to the job must be proven through a separate explanation letter.
A bootcamp completion certificate alone cannot substitute for the bachelor's requirement.

### Design and Arts Occupations

For product designer and visual designer codes, degrees in fine arts or design fields are the standard.
There are cases where 5+ years of relevant experience + a portfolio can serve as a substitute application.
In such cases, the commercial track record reflected in the portfolio becomes the core evidence.

### Interpretation/Translation and Overseas Sales Occupations

The structure typically combines a bachelor's degree in the relevant foreign language, or being a native of the relevant country, with documented Korean language proficiency.
For specialized languages like Chinese, Vietnamese, or Russian, a home-country bachelor's degree may actually be recognized more broadly than a Korean bachelor's.
This area varies in scope of recognition by occupational code, so prior review is necessary.

<h2 id="section-5">Education and Career Documentation and Certification Procedures</h2>

Education proof doesn't end with a simple graduation certificate.
Degrees issued in the home country must go through separate certification procedures to be recognized as valid.

### Methods of Degree Certification

- Degree certificate issued by the home country's government + apostille or consular confirmation
- For Korean degrees, the degree conferral certificate + original graduation certificate
- Educational credential evaluation reports (such as WES) — required additionally by some occupations

Degree title, major name, and graduation date must be precisely indicated.
Translations must be notarized or carry administrative agent translation certification to be recognized.

### Key Points for Writing the Employment Certificate

| Item | Must Include | Impact if Missing |
|---|---|---|
| Employment period | Start and end dates clearly stated | Risk of being treated as under 1 year |
| Position/title | Exact job title | Potential occupational mismatch |
| Job duties | Specific description of work | Major relevance may be rejected |
| Company information | Business registration, contact details | Doubts about company's existence |
| Issuer signature | Seal of HR officer in charge | Additional authenticity verification |

If the employment certificate contains nothing more than a one-line "confirmation of employment," supplementary requests almost always follow.
The key is to describe job duties specifically enough that they match the job description tied to the occupational code.

> **Note:** If career falsification is suspected, on-site investigation may be conducted through the Korean diplomatic mission in the relevant country. If the company has shut down or become unreachable, the entire career history can be invalidated — preparation in advance is necessary.

<h2 id="section-6">Patterns That Frequently Appear in Rejection Cases</h2>

Recently, cases where applicants had the necessary education and experience but were still rejected have been increasing.
The reasons usually narrow down to three.

### First: Weak Explanation of Major-to-Occupation Matching

When the major name on the graduation certificate doesn't intuitively connect to the duties of the occupational code, requests for supplementary materials follow.
Attaching just a one-line "related major" explanation rarely gets through.
You need to bundle course completion records, thesis topics, and relevant certifications together to construct a matching logic.

### Second: Errors in Calculating Career Duration

Whether probationary periods, internships, and part-time work count toward formal career experience often becomes an issue.
Without a record of social insurance enrollment, having career experience recognized is often difficult.
For home-country experience, social insurance enrollment proof under the home country's standards becomes decisive evidence.

### Third: Falling Short of Wage Requirements

It's common for applications to be rejected because, while education and experience were met, the salary offered by the employer fell below the wage requirement set for that occupation.
This standard is adjusted annually based on GNI changes, so the exact threshold at the time of application must be confirmed with the competent Immigration Office.
Annual changes are announced via the [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) notice board.

<h2 id="section-7">FAQ</h2>

**Q1. Without a bachelor's degree, is it impossible to apply for an E-7 visa?**

Even without a bachelor's degree, some occupations allow application with 5 or more years of relevant experience.
However, recognition varies by occupation, and the documentation burden becomes much heavier.
Whether your case fits the career-substitution track is something that needs to be reviewed against the specific occupational code first.

**Q2. If my major differs from the occupation, am I automatically rejected?**

Not automatically.
You can build a supplementary explanation by bundling a minor, double major, relevant certifications, completed coursework, and related work experience.
That said, IT, design, and specialized engineering codes apply strict major-matching review, so prior assessment is necessary.

**Q3. Between a home-country degree and a Korean degree, which is more advantageous?**

It depends on the occupation.
For general office and managerial roles, a Korean degree is faster through review, while for specialized-language interpretation/translation and overseas sales, a home-country degree is recognized more naturally.
Holding a Korean master's degree or higher opens a path where career requirements may be waived.

**Q4. If I can't obtain an employment certificate, are there alternatives?**

When the company has closed or refuses to issue one, you can combine social insurance enrollment proof, salary deposit records, tax payment records, and colleague affidavits as evidence.
However, the scope of recognition is narrower than with a formal employment certificate, so reviewing in advance how far this can go is necessary.

**Q5. Can students who are about to graduate apply for the E-7?**

In principle, application after degree conferral is the proper path.
Without an issued graduation certificate, applications typically get stuck at this stage.
For those about to graduate from a Korean university, the common route is to convert to a D-10 job-seeking visa first, then move to E-7 after the degree is issued.

**Q6. Where can I check E-7 occupational codes?**

Requirements by occupational code are posted on [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) and the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr) notices.
However, codes are frequently changed, added, or consolidated, so verifying the most recent notification as of your application date is necessary.
You can also review the body of the Immigration Control Act Enforcement Decree at the [Korean Law Information Center](https://www.law.go.kr).
