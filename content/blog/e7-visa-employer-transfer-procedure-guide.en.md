# Complete Guide to E-7 Visa Job Changes: Procedures and Practical Considerations

Changing jobs on an E-7 visa is not as simple as just switching employers.

Your new company's occupation code, revenue scale, and whether your education and experience match the new role are all re-examined from scratch.

This article walks through the practical roadblocks E-7 holders run into, from the moment they decide to change jobs through the issuance of the workplace change permit.

<h2 id="section-1">E-7 Visa Job Change: What to Check First</h2>

### Does the new company's occupation match your E-7 code?

E-7 visas are assigned codes across 85 designated activity occupations.

Even roles that look similar on the surface — like 'Management Consultant (2715)' and 'Overseas Sales Representative (2742)' — are entirely different occupations.

A job change may technically be possible, but if the occupation changes, the review becomes closer to an out-of-status activity permit or a status change rather than a simple workplace change.

The first thing to check is whether the business type registered on the new company's business registration certificate, and the duties you'll actually perform, fall within your existing E-7 code.

If this alignment is weak, no amount of supporting paperwork will save the application.

### Do your education and experience meet the new occupation's requirements?

E-7 has different education and experience requirements for each occupation.

Just because your previous employer accepted your credentials doesn't mean they'll be accepted under a new occupation.

The scope of recognized experience in particular varies by immigration office interpretation, so you should first check the occupation-specific guidance on [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) to see where your case lands.

> **Caution:** Even when the occupation code matches, if the new company is too small or its revenue falls short of the threshold, the application can still be denied within the same occupation.

<h2 id="section-2">The Mandatory 15-Day Reporting Rule for E-7 Job Changes</h2>

### Workplace Change Report vs. Workplace Change Permit

E-7 is, in principle, a visa that requires advance approval of a 'workplace change permit.'

This is different from visas like E-9, where a simple notification is sufficient.

Under Article 21 of the Immigration Act, changing your workplace without prior approval is subject to penalties.

You can review the exact statute on the [Korean Law Information Center](https://www.law.go.kr).

### What the 15-Day Number Actually Means

The 15-day rule is one of the most commonly misunderstood points in practice.

| Category | Timing | Process |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prior approval | After deciding to change jobs, before starting at the new company | Apply for advance immigration approval |
| Post-event report | Only for job changes due to certain exceptional reasons | Report to immigration within 15 days |
| If violated | Working without notification or approval | Fines, shortened stay, possible denial of extension |

The 15-day window only applies to a limited set of reasons where post-event reporting is allowed — the default is prior approval.

A common pitfall is starting work first and then trying to "report it later," which often unravels the entire case.

<h2 id="section-3">Documents Required for a Workplace Change Permit Application</h2>

### Documents the New Company Must Provide

Without cooperation from the new employer, the job change cannot move forward.

- Copy of business registration certificate
- Corporate registry extract
- Employment contract (specifying duties, salary, and contract period)
- Revenue documentation (VAT taxable standard certificate, etc.)
- List of enrollees in the four major insurances
- Company brochure or business overview materials

Revenue documentation in particular is the key evidence that the new company meets the scale required to hire an E-7 foreign worker.

If this is weak, no matter how strong your personal profile is, the application will be blocked at the company qualification stage.

### Documents You Must Prepare

- Passport and Alien Registration Card
- Integrated application form
- Degree and experience certificates (notarization or apostille may be required)
- Consent-to-transfer letter from current employer, or proof of resignation
- Four major insurance enrollment and loss-of-coverage confirmation from the previous company

When the previous employer refuses to cooperate, figuring out how to substitute proof of resignation is one of the most frequent sticking points in practice.

> **Practical Tip:** If you are in dispute with your previous employer, you can substitute objective evidence such as a copy of your resignation letter, certified content mail, or a receipt for an unpaid wage complaint.

<h2 id="section-4">Practical Differences by Reason and Timing of the Job Change</h2>

### Voluntary vs. Involuntary Job Changes

Voluntary job changes (salary raise, career move) and involuntary ones (company closure, layoff) are reviewed with different levels of scrutiny.

Involuntary changes are granted relatively wide latitude for post-event reporting, while voluntary changes default to requiring prior approval.

In practice, if the explanation for "why this company, why now" is weak, a request for supplementary documents will follow immediately.

### When Your Stay Permit Is About to Expire

If you change jobs with less than three months remaining on your stay, you'll need to process the workplace change and the extension at the same time.

In this scenario, a single missing document can block both applications.

Processing times vary by the immigration office of jurisdiction, and the fastest path for your specific situation requires case-by-case review.

<h2 id="section-5">Common Problems Behind Denied E-7 Job Change Cases</h2>

### Forcing a Mismatched Occupation to Fit

The most common reason for denial is a job description that has been artificially massaged to fit the applicant's E-7 code.

Even when it looks aligned on paper, if the company's actual business doesn't back it up, the discrepancy surfaces immediately during site inspections or follow-up document requests.

### Weak Revenue or Headcount at the New Company

E-7 also evaluates company size and the proportion of Korean nationals employed — a key difference from E-9.

If the company is a newly established entity with almost no Korean staff, or revenue is negligible, the application can be blocked on company qualifications even if your personal profile is excellent.

### Salary Below the Prior-Year GNI Threshold

E-7 has occupation-specific minimum salary requirements.

If your new salary falls below your previous one, that alone can disqualify you.

Salary thresholds change annually, so it's safer to confirm this year's exact figure through a consultation.

<h2 id="section-6">What Happens to Your Alien Registration Card and Stay Period After the Change?</h2>

### What Gets Updated After the Workplace Change Permit

When the workplace change permit is granted, the Alien Registration Card itself is not reissued.

What changes is the workplace information registered with immigration, which is updated to reflect the new employer.

In principle, your stay period's expiration date remains the same — any extension is handled as a separate procedure.

### Common Snags with Four Major Insurances and Taxes

| Item | Previous Employer | New Employer | Watch Out For |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Four major insurances | Loss-of-coverage filing | Acquisition filing | Gaps raise suspicion at immigration |
| Withholding tax | Year-end settlement on departure | New filing | Year-end settlement split between employers |
| Alien Registration Card | Subject to change notification | New employer info reflected | Card replacement is separate |

A gap of more than one month in insurance coverage becomes grounds for suspicion about whether you were actually working.

> **Caution:** If there's a gap between your start date and the date approval is issued, work during that period can be interpreted as unauthorized employment.

<h2 id="section-7">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

### Q1. Can I apply for the workplace change permit after I've already started the new job?

The default rule is prior approval.

Post-event reporting within 15 days is only recognized for reasons outside your control, such as company closure or layoffs.

If you treat a voluntary job change as something to "report later" after starting work, it can be interpreted as unauthorized employment and result in fines.

### Q2. Is it possible to move to a company with a different occupation code?

It's possible.

However, instead of a simple workplace change, the review effectively becomes a new E-7 status qualification process.

Your education and experience must meet the new occupation's requirements, and the new company must also be qualified to hire foreign nationals under that occupation.

### Q3. The company I want to join is newly established. Will it still work?

A job change to a recently founded company is possible.

That said, since revenue documentation will be weak, you'll need to bolster the company's qualifications with a business plan, transaction records, and proof of Korean national hires.

If this area is weak, supplementary document requests tend to drag on.

### Q4. My new salary is slightly lower than my current one. Is that okay?

If it's still above the minimum salary threshold for the occupation, it's fine.

If it drops below the threshold itself, that's a disqualification.

Thresholds are renewed annually, so this year's precise figure for your occupation needs case-by-case confirmation.

### Q5. My current employer is dragging their feet on processing my resignation. What can I do?

This is one of the most frequent sticking points in practice.

You can substitute objective evidence such as a resignation letter, certified content mail, or a complaint filed with the labor office.

The scope of what's accepted varies by case, so a review before proceeding is necessary.

### Q6. How long does processing usually take?

It varies significantly by the immigration office of jurisdiction.

The fastest cases take two to three weeks; if a supplementary document request comes in, it can stretch beyond two months.

Finding the fastest jurisdiction to file in is the key.
