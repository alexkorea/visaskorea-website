# HiKorea D-2 Part-Time Work Permission Violation Penalty Guide — What Actually Happens If You're Caught

If a D-2 international student works without part-time employment permission or steps outside the scope of what was approved, the consequences can range from fines and shortened stay periods to denied extensions and forced departure mid-studies.

This applies to all D-2-1 through D-2-8 language and degree students. Even a single shift at a part-time job without notification counts as a violation from that moment onward, regardless of whether the work relates to your studies.

This guide walks through violation types, how they get caught, the actual disposition process, recovery prospects, and the points where students still get stuck even after self-reporting — all based on how immigration offices handle these cases in practice.

<h2 id="section-1">What Counts as a D-2 Part-Time Work Permission Violation</h2>

The question isn't "did you work?" — it's "did you work without permission?"

D-2 is a student visa, so employment activity is prohibited by default and only unlocked through a separate notification.

The legal basis is [Article 20 of the Immigration Control Act (activities outside status)](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/출입국관리법) and the enforcement decree provisions on the part-time work exception for international students.

### Common Violation Types in Practice

The patterns most frequently flagged during review:

- Starting a part-time job without filing a permission request at all
- Having a permit, but working at a different business than the one listed on the application
- Working more than the approved weekly hour cap
- Doing work outside the approved job category (simple labor, cafés, restaurants, etc.)
- Continuing to work after TOPIK level, attendance, or GPA requirements drop below the threshold

They may look similar on the surface, but which category your case falls into significantly changes the severity of the disposition.

### Frequently Misunderstood Areas

The two most commonly missed scenarios are "unpaid internships" and "helping out at a friend's shop for a bit."

Whether compensated or not, providing labor at a business falls under [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) part-time work permission rules. Immigration determines the facts based on substance, even without a business card or written contract.

Enforcement priorities and the exact scope of what counts shift quarter to quarter, so it's safer to verify through consultation whether your specific case qualifies as "work."

<h2 id="section-2">How Violations Get Discovered — The Signals Immigration Sees</h2>

Most violations don't come to light through "reports" — they surface through other administrative records.

If you're not aware of this, you can end up standing in front of immigration with files already built against you without ever realizing it.

### The Most Common Detection Paths

- Enrollment history in the four major insurances (National Health Insurance, Employment Insurance)
- Day-labor and regular wage income data submitted by employers to the [National Tax Service](https://www.nts.go.kr)
- Your name appearing on the list during a worksite inspection
- Work activity inferred from your school's attendance and grade records during visa extension or change applications
- Statements obtained from other foreign employees during investigations at the same business

The four-insurance enrollment in particular can be filed by the employer without your consent, so your employment status gets linked into the immigration system without you knowing.

### Circumstances Immigration Reads as "Intentional"

In practice, officers distinguish between honest mistakes and intent.

- Working more than six months with zero trace of any permit application
- Long-term employment at a business different from the approved one
- Continuing to work even while facing academic probation or dismissal
- Prior history of being warned or sanctioned for the same violation

When two or more of these stack up, the case can escalate from a simple fine into review for forced deportation.

<h2 id="section-3">Actual Penalties for Violations — The Disposition Flow Stage by Stage</h2>

The point to understand is this:

A D-2 part-time work violation isn't "just pay and move on" — it leaves a mark on your stay record itself.

| Disposition Type | When It Applies | Impact on Stay |
|---|---|---|
| Warning | Minor, one-time, self-reported | Recorded; weighted review at next extension |
| Fine (notification disposition) | Standard unauthorized work | Extension/change can still be denied after payment |
| Departure order | More serious violations | Voluntary departure form; re-entry restricted for a period |
| Forced deportation | Repeated, intentional, severe violations | Entry ban of 5+ years possible |
| Parallel criminal proceedings | Referred to prosecution depending on facts | Fine sentence; affects all future visas |

The legal basis is [Article 94 of the Immigration Control Act (penal provisions)](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/출입국관리법) and Article 46 on deportation targets.

### Why It Doesn't End With the Fine

When you receive a fine notification, it can look like the matter is closed — but the immigration system retains it as a "law violation history."

This record resurfaces at moments like:

- D-2 stay period extension review
- D-10 job-seeker visa or E-7 employment visa conversion review
- F-2 point system or F-5 permanent residence application — good conduct requirements
- Future re-entry permits and visa issuance review

The most dangerous assumption is "I paid the fine, so I'm done."

### Shortened Stay Periods and Denied Extensions

Once a violation is confirmed, your remaining stay can be cut short, or the extension application itself can be denied.

In practice, students about to graduate and convert to D-10 often get blocked at exactly this stage.

The exact standards for denial and the recovery options depend on when the violation occurred, how long it lasted, and how much income was involved — so submitting an application without addressing your case first can shut the door in one shot.

> **Caution:** Filing an extension while hiding the violation creates a separate ground for disadvantage. If your insurance and tax data are already linked, "not getting caught" is effectively impossible.

<h2 id="section-4">When You Already Have a Violation on Record — Is Recovery Possible</h2>

What ultimately matters is whether a student with a violation history can finish their studies, and whether they can convert to a work visa after graduation.

### Variables That Decide Your Recovery Odds

| Variable | Works in Your Favor | Works Against You |
|---|---|---|
| Duration of violation | Short, 1–2 months | Long-term, 6+ months |
| Type of work | Simple labor — cafés, convenience stores | Adult entertainment, unregistered businesses |
| Academic status | Attendance and grades maintained | Academic probation or dismissal risk |
| Self-reporting | Self-reported before detection | Statement made after inspection |
| Prior history | First violation | Same violation repeated |

Each cell in this table effectively becomes a score in the extension and conversion review.

### When Self-Reporting Actually Helps

Many students give up, thinking "I already worked, so it's over." But self-reporting before detection genuinely operates as a factor that reduces the severity of the disposition.

That said, once you self-report you can't take it back, so you need to check first whether your case is actually one where self-reporting works in your favor.

- Whether your four-insurance enrollment is already on record
- Whether the employer is willing to file a report
- How much stay period you have remaining
- How close you are to graduation or conversion

If even one of these four points isn't aligned, self-reporting can backfire.

<h2 id="section-5">After a Penalty, Can You Still Convert to D-10 or E-7</h2>

This is the most common question we get.

"I just paid the fine — can I still get D-10 after graduation?"

### What Reviewers Actually Look At

- Time elapsed since the violation ended (measured in semesters)
- Degree of academic recovery after the violation (attendance, grades, graduation)
- TOPIK level and consistency between your major and job-search direction
- Whether the record has stayed clean with no further violations

When these elements are in place, the conversion path to D-10 — and onward to E-7 — isn't fundamentally blocked.

That said, even the same violation can produce different outcomes depending on the school, major, job-search direction, and remaining stay period, so it's hard to draw blanket conclusions.

<h2 id="section-6">When the Employer Is Caught Alongside You — The Student Isn't the Only One Penalized</h2>

D-2 part-time work violations don't only penalize the student.

Under [Article 18 of the Immigration Control Act (restrictions on foreign employment)](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/출입국관리법), employers who hire foreigners without permission also face separate fines and penalties.

### How the Employer's Disposition Affects the Student's Case

- When the employer's statement locks in the duration and hours of the student's violation as-is
- When the business closes or relocates, making it harder to prove or rebut the violation itself
- When statements from other foreign employees at the same business get bundled together

If the student trusts a "leave it to me, I'll handle it" assurance from the owner and gives a statement based on that, more damaging facts can end up locked in against them.

In the actual investigation phase, a single sentence in a statement often decides the disposition.

> **Practical tip:** If you receive an investigation notice or call from immigration, it's safer to organize your case before your first statement. Once a statement goes in, reversing it is extremely difficult.

<h2 id="section-7">FAQ</h2>

**Q1. I only helped out at a friend's shop for a few days — is that still a violation?**
Regardless of whether you were paid, providing labor at a business falls under part-time work permission. Even short-term, low-pay work can leave a violation record if a single line shows up in the four-insurance or tax data.

**Q2. I was working at a café I had permission for, but the owner sent me to a different branch. Is that okay?**
The permit is issued per business location. A different branch requires a separate filing, and moving without notification gets recorded as "work outside the approved scope."

**Q3. I paid the fine — will I still get my visa renewed at extension?**
Payment alone doesn't automatically restore your standing. The result depends on the violation period, your academic status, and remaining stay period, so a review is needed before filing rather than just submitting as-is.

**Q4. I want to convert to D-10 after graduation, but I have a past violation on record.**
It isn't a blanket disqualifier on its own. However, time elapsed since the violation, academic recovery, and job-search direction are all evaluated together, so timing and document strategy make a real difference.

**Q5. If I self-report, can I avoid forced deportation?**
This doesn't apply uniformly to all cases. If the violation isn't severe and you self-report before detection, the disposition may be reduced — but outcomes vary by the facts.

**Q6. I received an immigration investigation notice in English — how should I respond?**
The first statement is the stage that locks in the facts, so before responding you need to organize your violation type, duration, and the state of the evidence. The right direction varies by case and is hard to generalize.
