# How to Apply for an H-1 Working Holiday Visa: What Actually Decides the Outcome at Screening

The H-1 Working Holiday visa is a single-entry visa that allows young people from countries with a bilateral agreement with Korea to stay for one year, combining travel with short-term employment.

It is open to nationals of partner countries between the ages of 18 and 30 (with some countries setting the upper limit at 25 or 35), and applications must be submitted through the Korean embassy or consulate in your country of nationality — not at a Korean immigration office inside Korea.

This guide walks through eligibility, document preparation, the points where screening most often stalls, and how to manage your stay after arrival — focusing on what actually matters in practice.

<h2 id="section-1">Understanding What the H-1 Visa Really Is</h2>

### The Legal Character of a Working Holiday

H-1 is a long-term residence status under Annex 1-2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Immigration Act. Its original purpose is *holiday*, and *work* is treated as an incidental activity to cover living costs during the stay.

The order matters — and you have to look at it in that order first.

From a consular officer's perspective, H-1 is classified as a cultural-exchange visa, not an employment visa.

If this framing is weak in your application, your paperwork can read as employment-focused, which itself becomes grounds for refusal.

### Partner Countries and Age Limits

Korea currently has working holiday agreements with more than 20 countries, including Australia, Japan, Canada, New Zealand, Germany, and France.

The complete list of partner countries, country-specific age caps, and annual quotas is available on the official [Ministry of Foreign Affairs Working Holiday Info Center](https://whic.mofa.go.kr) page.

Some country quotas close out early in the year, so it's worth checking the remaining quota for your nationality separately before applying.

<h2 id="section-2">Eligibility — What to Verify Before Even Touching the Documents</h2>

### Basic Eligibility Requirements

The basic requirements for H-1 look straightforward, but the details are where actual screening decisions get made.

- Nationality of a partner country
- Age 18 to 30 at the time of application (varies by country)
- No accompanying dependents
- No prior H-1 visa issuance
- Good health, no criminal record
- Sufficient initial settlement funds for the stay

The key point is *"one-time only for the same visa."*

Anyone who has previously held a Korean working holiday visa cannot apply again.

### Where Financial Proof Most Often Trips People Up

On the surface, it looks like showing a bank balance should be enough — but in practice, the part that trips people up is explaining the *source* of those funds.

Submitting only an account under a parent's name often won't be accepted as the applicant's own funds.

Approval odds rise significantly when the account is in the applicant's own name, the balance has been maintained for a meaningful period, and the deposit history shows a natural flow.

The required amount varies by country and year, so we'll confirm the current threshold for your nationality during consultation.

<h2 id="section-3">Required Documents — Consistency Matters More Than Format</h2>

### Common Documents

| Document | Description | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Visa Application Form | Standard application | Applicant signature required |
| Passport | Minimum 6 months remaining validity | Separate copy required |
| Color Photo | Taken within the last 6 months | Must meet spec |
| Activity Plan | Plan of activities during stay | English or Korean |
| Financial Proof | Balance certificate in applicant's name | Recently issued |
| Round-trip Ticket or Additional Funds | Return ticket or funds to purchase one | Choose one |
| Health Certificate | Required by some countries only | From designated hospital |
| Criminal Background Check | Original from home country | Apostille or consular authentication |

Requirements vary slightly by country, and some consulates use their own internal forms.

### The Activity Plan: Persuasiveness Over Length

A long activity plan matters less than one that flows naturally.

What the consular officer is reading for is: *"How is this person actually going to live in Korea for a year?"*

Location, type of accommodation, ideas for short-term work, travel plans, intent to learn Korean — these should all connect consistently.

This is where applications separate.

If the plan over-emphasizes the intent to work, it can be read as drifting from the H-1's underlying purpose.

<h2 id="section-4">Application Process Step by Step</h2>

### From Submission to Arrival

| Step | Action | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Step 1 | Apply at the Korean embassy/consulate in your country of nationality | Overseas mission |
| Step 2 | Document review and interview (if requested) | Consular officer |
| Step 3 | Visa issuance or request for additional materials | Overseas mission |
| Step 4 | Collect passport with visa affixed | Applicant or representative |
| Step 5 | Enter Korea | Immigration inspection on arrival |
| Step 6 | Register as a foreign resident if staying over 90 days | Local Immigration Office |

Processing time varies by mission — typically 2 to 4 weeks on average.

It can run longer during peak periods, so we recommend applying at least two months before your planned arrival date.

### Can You Reapply After a Refusal?

Common reasons for refusal include insufficient funds, inconsistencies in the activity plan, and prior immigration violations.

Reapplication after refusal is possible if the underlying issue is genuinely addressed — but resubmitting the same materials will result in the same refusal.

In fact, reapplying without correctly identifying the refusal reason tends to be the costliest waste of time.

> **Practical tip:** The reasons stated on a refusal notice are usually written in generic language, so the actual sticking point often isn't clear. A pre-application review is much faster than fixing things after a refusal.

<h2 id="section-5">What's Easy to Overlook After Arrival</h2>

### Foreign Resident Registration and Stay Obligations

If you stay on H-1 for more than 90 days, you must register as a foreign resident at the local Immigration Office within 90 days of entry.

Registration procedures and required documents are listed on the official [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) portal.

Missing this deadline triggers an administrative fine and works against you later if you apply for a status change or extension.

### Permitted and Restricted Lines of Work

H-1 allows short-term employment, but not in every sector.

Entertainment, gambling, and adult-industry-related work are explicitly prohibited.

Conversational English instruction — the territory of the E-2 visa — is also restricted under H-1 as a general rule.

If the nature of the work is borderline, failing to verify it before arrival can result in an order to cease the activity.

The detailed list of prohibited and allowed sectors is adjusted somewhat each year, so it's safest to confirm in advance whether your intended field is permitted.

### Extensions and Status Changes

H-1 is, in principle, a one-year single-entry visa that cannot be extended.

That said, some countries' agreements allow a six-month extension, and this must be written into the specific bilateral agreement.

If you want to stay in Korea after the H-1 period ends, you can consider switching to D-10 (job-seeking), E-2, E-7, or another status — though direct conversion from H-1 is significantly restricted.

Whether a status change is feasible depends on your work history, education, and whether you have a job offer from a Korean employer.

<h2 id="section-6">Points Frequently Missed During Screening</h2>

### Why More Paperwork Doesn't Mean Higher Approval

Volume of documents doesn't raise your approval odds.

In actual screening, what gets looked at first is consistency in the activity plan, clarity of fund sources, and a clean immigration history.

If even one of these three is weak, you'll get a request for additional documents no matter how thick the file is.

### Handling an Interview When One Is Scheduled

Some consulates require interviews on a random basis or when documents raise questions.

The interviews aren't long, but the core questions are: *"Why Korea?"*, *"What will you do for the year?"*, and *"What are your plans after the visa ends?"*

Statements that align with your written activity plan matter more than rehearsed answers.

If your account wavers here, the credibility of the entire file drops.

> **Caution:** If it becomes apparent that the working holiday is essentially being used as a workaround for employment or language study, that itself becomes grounds for refusal. Under the Immigration Act, activities that diverge from the visa's intended purpose can result in deportation if discovered later. Relevant statutes can be checked at the [Korean Law Information Center](https://law.go.kr).

<h2 id="section-8">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

**Q1. Can the H-1 Working Holiday visa be applied for from within Korea?**

In principle, you must apply at a Korean embassy or consulate located in your country of nationality.

H-1 visas are not issued by immigration offices inside Korea.

**Q2. If I previously stayed in Korea on a language student visa (D-4), will that affect my H-1 application?**

It is not a direct disqualifier on its own, but any record of immigration violations during the previous stay will affect the review.

If you departed normally with no violations, it generally isn't a problem.

**Q3. Can I work full-time in Korea on an H-1 visa?**

The H-1's intended purpose is holiday, and work is treated as an incidental activity.

Activity premised on long-term full-time employment runs counter to that intent and can be deemed out-of-status if discovered.

**Q4. How long does the visa typically take to issue?**

It varies by consulate, but averages 2 to 4 weeks — longer during peak periods.

If a request for supplemental documents is issued, additional time is needed, so it's safest to apply with a buffer.

**Q5. After an H-1 refusal, can I reapply for a different visa instead?**

Yes.

That said, if the refusal was based on insufficient funds or an inconsistent activity plan, those same factors can affect other visa categories too, so a review before reapplying is advisable.

**Q6. Do I really need to register as a foreign resident after arriving in Korea?**

It is mandatory if you'll stay more than 90 days.

If you plan to leave within 90 days, the registration requirement doesn't arise — but in daily life (opening a bank account, getting a mobile plan), an alien registration card is often necessary.
