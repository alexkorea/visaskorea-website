# E-9 to F-5 Permanent Residency Transition Guide

## Can E-9 Holders Obtain F-5 Permanent Residency?

The E-9 (Non-Professional Employment) visa is designed for foreign workers in manufacturing, agriculture, fisheries, and construction. Historically, E-9 holders faced a nearly closed door to permanent residency. However, recent amendments to Korea's immigration regulations have opened **structured pathways** that allow long-term E-9 workers to eventually qualify for F-5 permanent residency.

The key caveat: there is **no direct E-9 → F-5 conversion**. The standard route requires intermediate visa status changes — most commonly through the **E-7-4 (Skilled Industrial Personnel)** visa category.

---

## Main Pathways from E-9 to F-5

### Pathway 1: E-9 → E-7-4 (Skilled Industrial Personnel) → F-5

This is the most commonly used and realistic route for E-9 holders.

**Conditions to convert E-9 to E-7-4:**
- Currently holding E-9 or H-2 (Working Visit) status
- Minimum **4 years of work experience** in the same industry sector
- Employer recommendation or recognized industrial skills qualification
- Korean language ability: TOPIK Level 2 or higher, or completion of KIIP (Social Integration Program) Stage 3

**Conditions to convert E-7-4 to F-5:**
- Continuous employment maintained under E-7-4 status
- Total lawful stay in Korea of **10 years or more**
- Meets income requirements and has no criminal record

### Pathway 2: E-9 → F-2-99 (Special Residency) → F-5

F-2-99 is a discretionary residency status granted at the Minister of Justice's discretion for individuals with exceptional social contribution or national interest significance. This pathway is very rare and not applicable to most E-9 workers.

### Pathway 3: Marriage to Korean National → F-6 → F-5

If an E-9 holder marries a Korean national, they can convert to F-6 (Marriage Immigration) status. After maintaining the marriage for 2 years (or having children), they may qualify for F-5 permanent residency through the marriage-based route.

---

## 10-Year Residency Requirement

F-5 permanent residency generally requires **10 or more years of lawful stay in Korea**. Key points to understand:

- **Extended absences reduce your counted stay.** A single absence exceeding **90 consecutive days** or cumulative absences exceeding **180 days per year** can negatively impact your residency count.
- Multiple visa status changes do not reset the clock — lawful periods under all statuses are **accumulated**.
- **Illegal stays are excluded** from the count and constitute grounds for disqualification. Even a single day of overstay can affect your application.

Practical advice: Request an official entry/exit records certificate from HiKorea or your local immigration office and verify your total lawful stay before starting the application process.

---

## Criminal Record Standards

Your criminal history is one of the most heavily weighted factors in F-5 review.

- **Imprisonment or heavier sentence:** Permanent residency is generally denied.
- **Fines:** A single fine under KRW 5 million may be considered with context, but repeated violations are viewed unfavorably.
- **Immigration violations:** Unauthorized job changes, illegal stays, or working without proper authorization are significant disqualifying factors.
- **Recommended action:** Obtain a **Criminal Record Certificate (from the National Police Agency)** and an **Immigration Record Certificate** before applying to identify any issues in advance.

---

## Income Requirements

F-5 applicants must demonstrate financial self-sufficiency.

- Applicants must show **stable income at or above the minimum wage** standard.
- As of 2026, Korea's minimum wage is KRW 10,030/hour, equivalent to approximately KRW 2,096,270/month.
- Income significantly below this threshold — particularly if it qualifies you for basic welfare assistance — is treated as a negative factor in the review.
- Acceptable documentation includes employment income certificates, withholding tax receipts, and tax office income confirmations. Rental or dividend income may also be counted.

## Required Documents

### Common Documents for All Applicants
- Passport and Alien Registration Card (ARC) copy
- Application form (official Immigration Office form)
- 1 color passport photo (3.5×4.5 cm)
- Application fee receipt (KRW 200,000)

### Additional Documents for E-7-4 Route F-5 Applicants
- E-7-4 status verification documents
- Entry/exit records certificate confirming 10+ years of lawful stay
- Employment confirmation letter or certificate of employment
- Withholding tax receipt or income confirmation (most recent 12 months)
- National Health Insurance premium payment certificate
- Tax compliance certificate (issued by tax office)
- Criminal Record Certificate (issued by National Police Agency)
- Korean language proficiency proof (TOPIK certificate or KIIP completion certificate)

## Application Process

### Step 1: Determine Your Pathway
Review your current visa status and total time in Korea. If E-7-4 conversion is needed first, address that before starting F-5 preparation.

### Step 2: Pre-Review Consultation (Optional but Recommended)
The Ministry of Justice offers a pre-screening consultation service. Speaking with an immigration officer before submitting documents can save significant time and effort.

### Step 3: Prepare and Authenticate Documents
Foreign-language documents must be translated into Korean and notarized. Use a certified translation professional or the Korean embassy in your home country.

### Step 4: Submit the Application
- **In person:** Visit the immigration office that has jurisdiction over your address
- **Online:** Apply through HiKorea (www.hikorea.go.kr)

### Step 5: Await Review and Receive Decision
- Processing time: typically **1–3 months**
- If additional documents are requested, respond promptly
- Upon approval: receive F-5 status change permit and new F-5 Alien Registration Card

## Important Cautions

**1. Unauthorized Job Changes**
Any history of leaving your workplace without proper authorization during E-9 status is a red flag for both E-7-4 conversion and F-5 review. If you have such a record, consult an immigration professional before applying.

**2. Never Allow Your Visa to Expire**
A single day of overstay can complicate your entire application. Apply for extension or status change well before your current status expires.

**3. Health Insurance Payment Record**
Prolonged non-payment of National Health Insurance premiums is viewed negatively. Clear any arrears before applying.

**4. Limit Extended Absences**
Absences exceeding 180 cumulative days per year can reduce your counted lawful stay and delay your eligibility for F-5.

**5. Employer Cooperation Is Essential**
The E-7-4 pathway requires employer recommendations or employment confirmation. If you plan to change employers, factor this into your transition timeline carefully.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. If I've been in Korea on E-9 for 10 years, can I apply directly for F-5?**
No. There is no direct E-9 to F-5 conversion path. You must first transition to an intermediate visa status — most commonly E-7-4 — before becoming eligible for F-5.

**Q2. I've changed workplaces several times. Does my experience still count?**
Experience within the same industry sector can generally be aggregated for E-7-4 purposes. However, any unauthorized job departures in your record will be reviewed critically. Always change workplaces through proper legal procedures.

**Q3. My Korean language ability is limited. What can I do?**
The **KIIP (Social Integration Program)** is an alternative to TOPIK. Completing KIIP Stage 3 is accepted as equivalent to TOPIK Level 2. Classes are offered free of charge at centers throughout Korea.

**Q4. Can my family get permanent residency too?**
Once you hold F-5, your spouse and unmarried minor children can apply for **F-5 Dependent status** or **F-3 (Dependent)** to remain in Korea with you.

**Q5. What happens if my application is denied?**
You can reapply after addressing the reasons for denial. You may also file an official objection within 30 days of receiving the denial notice. Most denials related to income shortfalls or document deficiencies can be resolved through a corrected reapplication.
