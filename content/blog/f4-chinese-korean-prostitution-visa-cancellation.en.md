# Practical Guide to Visa Cancellation and Entry Ban When Korean-Chinese F-4 Holders Are Caught for Prostitution

When a Korean-Chinese F-4 visa holder is caught for prostitution, even a light fine often leads to visa cancellation, deportation, and an entry ban at the immigration stage.

This applies to Chinese nationals of Korean descent residing in Korea under the Overseas Korean (F-4) status. The criminal case ending does not mean the matter is closed — a separate immigration review (사범심사, *sabeom-simsa*) is conducted independently.

This article covers the immediate aftermath of being caught, the decisive points in the immigration review, the criteria for visa cancellation and entry bans, what can actually be contested, and the possibility of re-entry.

<h2 id="section-1">What Goes Wrong First When a Korean-Chinese F-4 Holder Is Caught for Prostitution</h2>

### Criminal Punishment and Immigration Action Are Separate Tracks

The first thing to understand is that criminal punishment and immigration action operate completely independently.

Many people feel safe once the prosecutor's office ends the case with a fine or suspended indictment.

In practice, however, once the criminal case concludes, immigration separately opens a review (*sabeom-simsa*) to decide whether to revoke residence status.

If this stage is mishandled, what looked like "just a fine" can suddenly turn into a departure order.

### Why F-4 Holders Face Tougher Treatment Than Other Visa Holders

In fact, F-4 cases are often handled more strictly in the immigration review than ordinary work visas.

[The Act on the Immigration and Legal Status of Overseas Koreans](https://www.law.go.kr), Article 5, expressly restricts granting of status to those who harm public order or good morals.

Prostitution falls under the [Act on the Punishment of Arrangement of Commercial Sex Acts, etc.](https://www.law.go.kr), which directly ties into the "morals violation" category.

On that basis, the F-4 status itself is typically re-examined from scratch.

<h2 id="section-2">How the Immigration Review Actually Unfolds</h2>

### Detection → Criminal Case → Notification to Immigration → Sabeom-simsa

| Stage | Authority | Key Point |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| Stage 1 — Detection | Police | Crackdown, investigation, written statement |
| Stage 2 — Criminal Case | Prosecution | Fine, suspended indictment, summary order |
| Stage 3 — Notification | Prosecution → Immigration | Outcome auto-forwarded |
| Stage 4 — Sabeom-simsa | Local Immigration Office | Decision on residence status revocation |
| Stage 5 — Disposition | Immigration | Cancellation, departure order, deportation, entry ban |

This is where outcomes diverge.

Many people only realize the weight of the situation when the Stage 4 summons for the immigration review arrives.

### Key Factors Reviewed in the Sabeom-simsa

In practice, immigration looks at the following:

- Nature of the act (one-off vs. repeated/habitual)
- What was said in statements at the time of detection
- Length of stay in Korea and family ties
- Prior offenses of the same kind
- Remorse, settlement, and measures to prevent recurrence

More than the length of your written response, how you address each of these items determines the severity of the disposition.

> **Warning:** What you say on the scene at the time of detection effectively determines more than half of the later immigration review outcome. Statements made during the criminal stage are passed on to immigration as-is.

<h2 id="section-3">How the Criteria for Visa Cancellation and Entry Bans Are Drawn</h2>

### Departure Order vs. Deportation vs. Entry Ban

| Disposition | Nature | Entry Ban |
|-------------|--------|-----------|
| Departure Order | Voluntary-departure format | Typically 1–5 years |
| Deportation | Forcible enforcement | Typically 5–10 years |
| Status Cancellation + Loss of Residence Permit | F-4 nullified | Separate entry ban imposed |

The core point is this.

Even within the same "prostitution" category, the outcome differs sharply depending on whether it is a single incident or involves repeated conduct or arrangement/brokering.

### How the Length of the Entry Ban Is Set

[The Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr), Articles 11 and 46, are the governing provisions.

Entry ban periods for morals violations are adjusted according to the seriousness of the case and prior offenses of the same type.

Internal guidelines are updated annually, so the exact period applicable to your case should be confirmed through a consultation.

In particular, involvement in arranging or advertising lengthens the period substantially.

<h2 id="section-5">What Can Actually Be Contested in the Sabeom-simsa</h2>

### Reversing Statements Almost Never Works

A common mistake is trying to overturn statements from the criminal stage during the immigration review.

In actual reviews, police and prosecution interrogation records are treated as the most reliable evidence.

Outcomes are usually better when you accept the statements themselves but explain the **nature of the act, the motive, and whether it was repeated** in concrete terms.

### Materials That Can Be Recognized as Mitigating Factors

In practice, the following materials carry real weight:

- Proof of family ties and life base in Korea (for long-term residents)
- Criminal record certificate showing no prior offenses of the same type
- Objective records of employment and income
- A written apology and a pledge against recurrence
- Documentation on Korean-resident direct family (children, spouse)

Even a thick stack of papers carries little weight if it is not tied to the core grounds.

### Possibility of Regaining F-4 Status Itself

This is the most frequently asked question.

The end of an entry ban does not automatically restore F-4 status.

When you apply for Overseas Korean status through [Hi Korea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr), the morals violation history is reviewed again.

The likelihood of regaining status depends on prior same-type offenses, the timing of entry ban lifting, and family ties in Korea, so an individual review is required.

<h2 id="section-6">Points That Trip People Up in Practice</h2>

### The Criminal Stage and the Immigration Stage Are Viewed Separately

> **Practice tip:** Criminal defense and immigration review require different strategies. A statement that worked in your favor in the criminal case can work against you in immigration.

This is especially true for statements about whether the act occurred, how many times, and where it took place.

Even if a confession helped reduce the criminal fine, the same confession becomes adverse material in the immigration review.

### What Blocks Re-Entry After Leaving

There are cases where entry is denied at the airport even after the entry ban period has passed.

This usually happens when no separate application to lift the entry ban was filed, or when the morals violation history remains on file at the status-grant stage.

Dispositions held by the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr) do not expire automatically.

### Impact on Accompanying Family Members

If the F-4 holder receives a disposition, accompanying family members' residence is also affected by chain reaction.

The residence status of a spouse and children is linked to the principal's status, so revocation triggers a re-examination of theirs as well.

Because outcomes here depend on the case and family composition, this should be addressed together before any disposition is finalized.

<h2 id="section-7">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

**Q1. I only paid a fine, but immigration is still contacting me.**

A prosecution disposition and the immigration sabeom-simsa are separate procedures.

Even a light fine does not stop immigration from independently deciding whether to revoke residence status.

**Q2. What happens if I do not attend the sabeom-simsa after receiving the summons?**

Failure to appear leads to the most adverse outcome in almost every case.

It is treated as waiving your chance to explain, and the likelihood of deportation rises.

**Q3. Does voluntary departure shorten the entry ban?**

Voluntary departure tends to carry a shorter entry ban than deportation.

That said, depending on the seriousness of the case, even a voluntary-departure format can carry a long ban, so prior review is essential.

**Q4. After F-4 cancellation, can I re-enter on a different visa?**

During the entry ban period, no visa of any kind allows entry.

Even after the ban is lifted, the morals violation history is reviewed during visa issuance, so the outcome depends on visa type and family ties.

**Q5. I have a child in Korea — is deportation automatic in that case?**

Family circumstances such as a minor child in Korea or a Korean-national spouse are considered as mitigating factors.

However, they do not automatically result in exemption — the outcome depends on the documents submitted and the way the case is explained.

**Q6. I had a lawyer for the criminal case — can I use the same person for the sabeom-simsa?**

Criminal defense and immigration review apply different laws and standards of judgment.

Because immigration dispositions are administrative procedures, review by a certified administrative agent (haengjeongsa) or administrative specialist with experience in sabeom-simsa, administrative appeals, and administrative litigation is more appropriate.
