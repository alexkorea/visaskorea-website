# HiKorea D-2 Part-Time Work Permission Violation Penalty Guide — Fines & Residence Impact

When D-2 international students work without part-time work permission, or exceed the approved hours or industry scope, they're treated as violators of the Immigration Control Act — facing fines, residence permit cancellation, and even deportation.

This issue applies equally to undergraduate and graduate students, language trainees (D-4) who have transitioned to D-2, and students moving to D-10 immediately after graduation.

We'll walk through the actual penalty severity, how violations get caught, school and immigration responses, and the long-term visa impact — all in one place.

<h2 id="section-1">The Basic Framework of D-2 Part-Time Work Permission</h2>

The D-2 visa is fundamentally a study-purpose residence status, so employment activities are restricted in principle.

To work part-time, you must obtain part-time work permission (permission for activities outside the residence status) in advance through [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr).

Here's the key point: working even one hour without permission is a violation, and stepping outside the approved hours or industry is also a violation.

### Scope of Activities Requiring Permission

Restaurant serving, convenience store cashier work, café part-time jobs, translation/interpretation freelancing, tutoring, and delivery riding all fall under part-time work permission.

Even on-campus work-study positions may require permission depending on how the school's work contract is structured, so it's safer to check in advance.

In particular, platform-based work like SNS advertising, YouTube monetization, and Coupang Flex often leads students into violations because they don't recognize them as "employment."

### Statutes Applied When Working Without Permission

The legal basis is Articles 18 (Restrictions on Foreign Employment) and 20 (Activities Outside Residence Status) of the [Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr).

Violations are punished under Article 94 (Penalties) or Article 100 (Administrative Fines) of the same act.

> **Caution:** This can become a criminal matter — it's not simply a "pay a small fine and move on" issue.

<h2 id="section-2">Penalty Severity by Violation Type</h2>

Even within the umbrella of "permission violations," the disposition varies based on how the violation occurred.

In practice, the following four are the most common.

| Violation Type | Main Disposition | Residence Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Working without applying for part-time work permission | Fine (notification disposition) or criminal booking | Possible residence period shortening or extension restriction |
| Exceeding approved hours (e.g., 20 hrs/week) | Fine + warning | Stricter review at next extension |
| Working in non-approved industry | Fine + activity permission cancellation | Re-review of D-2 status itself |
| Working in prohibited industries (entertainment, etc.) | Criminal booking + deportation | Possible entry ban |

### How a Fine (Notification Disposition) Actually Flows

Minor violations are handled by notification disposition with a fine, and payment closes the case without criminal proceedings.

If you refuse to pay or the matter is more serious, it escalates to summary judgment or criminal trial.

### Residence Permit Cancellation or Shortening

When violations are repeated or come paired with poor academic performance, the D-2 status itself becomes shaky.

Immigration offices evaluate violations alongside attendance and grades — so a single part-time job violation can balloon into suspicion of poor academic standing.

If this is weak, you may end up receiving a mid-semester residence shortening notice and having to schedule your departure.

<h2 id="section-3">How Violations Get Caught — Where You Get Exposed</h2>

Even if you think "I won't get caught," there are surprisingly many detection paths.

### Exposure Through the Employer

The most common path is workplace inspections and tax records.

When an employer files national insurance reports or submits daily wage payment statements, foreign employment information is automatically logged into the system.

In fact, many cases of "D-2 working without permission" surface through properly reported jobs.

### Your Own SNS and Bank Records

Delivery app settlement records, KakaoBank/Toss transfer history, and part-time job photos posted to Instagram become decisive evidence.

In real investigations, even when someone testifies "I only did it for a month," their statement collapses when bank flows reveal six months of activity.

### School Attendance and Academic Linkages

D-2 is linked to the school's international student management system and to immigration.

If a student with low attendance or on a leave of absence is found to have been working, it expands beyond a simple part-time violation into grounds for status unsuitability.

There's also a steady stream of cases where the school's international office reports students to immigration.

<h2 id="section-5">Visa Impact After Punishment</h2>

Don't relax thinking it's over once you've paid the fine.

The real problem surfaces at the next visa stage.

### Stricter Review for D-2 Extension/Change

Violation records remain in immigration's internal database, and they carry over directly into your next D-2 extension or D-10/E-7 conversion review.

Reviewers read the record as a "violation of the duty of diligent residence," so passing becomes harder even with the same documents.

In particular, when applying for the D-10 job-seeking visa after graduation, a part-time violation record is one of the first items checked.

### A Decisive Mark on Permanent Residency (F-5) and Naturalization

For students aiming long-term at [F-5 permanent residency](https://www.immigration.go.kr) or naturalization, a part-time violation record remains a decisive blemish.

A record of "duty-of-compliance violation" becomes a point deduction or rejection ground in both the points system and discretionary review.

What you need to look at first isn't "the fine now" but "the permanent residency review five years from now."

### Possibility of Deportation and Entry Ban

Working at entertainment establishments, part-time work linked to drugs or prostitution, and repeated violations are all grounds for deportation.

Once deportation is decided, an entry ban period (usually 1, 5, or 10 years) is imposed, blocking re-entry after graduation altogether.

> **Practice Tip:** Even when a violation exists, voluntarily reporting, giving honest testimony, and immediately stopping work can lower the disposition severity in some cases.

<h2 id="section-6">What to Do First When You Suspect a Violation</h2>

If you've already started working or have exceeded the permitted scope, the order of steps matters.

| Step | What to Do | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Step 1 | Stop working immediately | Don't accept any additional pay |
| Step 2 | Organize work records and pay history | Do not arbitrarily delete |
| Step 3 | Check the situation with the school's international office | Review before any unilateral self-reporting |
| Step 4 | Decide on a response plan after expert consultation | Must review before testimony |
| Step 5 | If needed, file a formal part-time work permission application | Transition to legal employment going forward |

### Voluntary Reporting vs. Wait-and-See Strategy

Voluntary reporting isn't always advantageous.

The strategy splits based on detection likelihood, level of evidence, and remaining residence period.

This judgment is hard to make alone, and there are real cases where misguided voluntary reporting actually triggers a criminal booking.

### Key Points for Writing Statements and Explanations

For statements, the key isn't writing at length — it's organizing the facts precisely by time, amount, and frequency.

Phrases like "I didn't know" or "my friend told me to" actually work against you.

For the explanation document, the practical point is to include mitigating factors — academic diligence, family support circumstances, tuition-funding background — together with concrete examples.

<h2 id="section-7">FAQ — Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

### Q1. Will I be punished even if I worked just one day without part-time work permission?

In principle, even one day is a violation.

That said, the disposition severity varies depending on the gravity of the matter and whether you self-report.

The possible disposition for your specific situation needs to be confirmed through consultation.

### Q2. Is exceeding the approved hours by even a little also a violation?

Yes, exceeding approved hours is also a violation.

The allowed hours differ during vacation versus during the semester, and standards differ between undergraduate and graduate students, so you need to recheck based on your own enrollment and semester status.

For the exact hour standards that apply this year, it's safer to check the [HiKorea announcements](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) for any changes before consulting.

### Q3. If the employer didn't file national insurance, won't I get caught?

It's actually more dangerous.

Failure to report is also a penalty for the employer, so during a workplace inspection it gets exposed all at once.

It's common to be caught after the fact via your bank deposit records, in-store CCTV, or coworker testimony.

### Q4. With a violation record, can I still convert to D-10 or E-7?

It's possible, but not straightforward.

The outcome depends on the type and duration of the violation, payment status, academic grades, and the level of the company hiring you after graduation.

A conversion strategy tailored to your case needs separate review.

### Q5. Does the school always report to immigration if they find out?

Internal rules differ by school.

Depending on the university, some end it with academic probation, while others make immigration notification their default policy.

It's safer to set your response order by examining both your school's regulations and immigration's processing direction together.

### Q6. Can I still extend my visa after paying the fine?

Paying the fine itself doesn't automatically block visa extension.

However, since the violation remains in the review record, you need to submit explanation documents together with your extension application to improve your chances of approval.
