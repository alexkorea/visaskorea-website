# H-2 Visa Eligibility for Overseas Koreans: Where Real Reviews Actually Get Decided

H-2 visa eligibility for overseas Koreans applies to foreign nationals of Korean descent aged 18 or older who can prove a direct lineage tie or blood relationship to Korea.
The main beneficiaries are ethnic Koreans residing in China and the former Soviet Union (CIS), and what sets this visa apart is that it allows employment — not just visits — under a multiple-entry sticker.
This article walks through how the eligibility rules are actually applied, where document preparation most often stalls, the issuance process, and how to reduce the odds of a rejection.

<h2 id="section-1">Who Falls Under the H-2 Visa</h2>

The H-2 visa primarily covers overseas Koreans who do not qualify for the F-4 (Overseas Korean) visa.
Under Appended Table 1-2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Immigration Act, applicants must be at least 18 years old, and the visa permits employment in unskilled-labor categories.
In practice, even among people who are clearly "of Korean descent," there is real confusion over whether they should be aiming at F-4 or H-2.

### The Fork Between F-4 and H-2

The first thing to check is whether you meet the education, qualification, or career thresholds.
A four-year university degree, a national technical certification, or experience as a corporate representative can put you on the F-4 track.
If none of those apply, you typically end up on the H-2 side.
Pushing for F-4 when you don't really qualify can actually hurt you — a rejection makes the next application harder — so picking the right track from the start, based on your actual profile, is the key.

### The Age 18 Threshold

H-2 requires the applicant to be 18 or older on the date of application.
Minor children cannot accompany a parent on H-2; they need a separate Visiting Cohabitation (F-1) visa route.
Miss this point and your whole family-relocation plan can unravel.

<h2 id="section-2">Core Requirements for H-2 Eligibility</h2>

Eligibility on paper is one thing — in actual review, the deciding question is "how do you prove you are of Korean descent?"
Even thick stacks of documents will get tripped up here if the blood relationship isn't shown clearly.

### How Lineage Is Actually Proven

You must meet the definition of an overseas Korean under Article 2 of the [Act on the Immigration and Legal Status of Overseas Koreans](https://www.law.go.kr).
In practice, lineage is established with documents such as:

- Proof that a parent or grandparent held Korean nationality
- Family registers (호적등본), removed family registers (제적등본), and family relationship certificates
- The applicant's birth certificate and proof of relationship to the parent
- A statement explaining how foreign nationality was acquired

If names are written inconsistently across these documents — between hanja, hangul, and the local language — you'll get a supplementation request almost immediately.
For ethnic Koreans from China especially, mismatches between the Chinese household register (hukou) and the Korean removed-register entries are very common.

### Work Visit Lottery and Invitation Requirements

For ethnic Koreans from China and CIS countries, issuance happens within the annual Work Visit Program quota.
There are two main routes:

| Route | Who it's for | Notes |
|------|------|------|
| Invitation by a domestic relative | Relatives in Korea aged 18+ (blood relatives within the 8th degree, in-laws within the 4th degree) | Inviter's eligibility is screened separately |
| Korean language test + computerized lottery | Those without a qualifying relative to invite them | Quotas and schedule shift year to year |

Because both the quota and the test schedule change yearly, which track is realistically open to you this year is something to confirm with the relevant authority.

<h2 id="section-3">Required Documents and Common Snags</h2>

The document list is published, but how each piece gets accepted varies a lot from case to case.

### Standard Submission Packet

| Document | Purpose | Notes |
|------|------|------|
| Visa application form | Standard template | Use the [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) form |
| Passport copy and photo | Identity verification | At least 6 months of remaining validity |
| Proof of Korean descent | Core to eligibility | Lineage chain must be consistent |
| Identity guarantee | Sponsor's guarantee | Only for the invitation track |

> **Heads up:** If issued documents are too old (typically over 3 months), you'll be asked to reissue them. Rather than collecting everything in one trip back home, time the issuance close to your application date.

### What Trips People Up in Practice

The most-missed point is consistent spelling of names.
The name has to match across Korean removed registers, your home-country ID, your passport, and your academic records — in hanja, hangul, and Latin letters alike.
Even one discrepancy means you'll need an additional "same person" certificate, and if that supporting paperwork is weak, the review drags out.

> **Practical tip:** If you anticipate name mismatches, getting a notarized identity-confirmation document in your home country up front is usually the fastest fix.

<h2 id="section-4">Issuance Procedure and Processing Times</h2>

The process itself is straightforward, but the timing varies step by step.

### Application Flow

1. Eligibility review and track decision (F-4 vs. H-2)
2. Apply for the visa through the consulate in your home country or via [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr)
3. Review and submission of any supplementary documents
4. Visa issued, entry into Korea
5. Within 90 days of entry, complete foreign resident registration and report start of employment

Processing times differ by consulate and by immigration office.
Even with the same paperwork, the frequency of supplementation requests varies by office, so real-world processing times can stretch to two or three times longer in some cases.

### Post-Entry Employment Steps

H-2 isn't a visa that ends the moment you receive the sticker.
After entry, you must complete employment training, register as a job seeker, and report the start of your employment before you can legally work.
Skipping any of these steps will catch up with you at the first stay-extension review.

<h2 id="section-5">Why H-2 Visas Get Rejected — and How to Lower the Risk</h2>

Rejections come from "insufficient explanation" more often than from "insufficient eligibility."

### Common Grounds for Rejection

- Unresolved name inconsistencies in lineage documents
- Past records of unlawful stay or deportation
- Inviter doesn't qualify, or the invitation is suspected to be fraudulent
- Failure to disclose a criminal record in the home country
- Inconsistencies in how the visa application form is filled out

If you have prior immigration violations in Korea, the outcome tends to hinge less on the violation itself and more on whether you disclosed it honestly.
Trying to hide it and being found out makes a re-application significantly harder.

### Re-application Strategy After a Rejection

Don't stop at the surface reason code and phrasing on the rejection notice.
In real review, the stated reason often comes paired with other underlying weaknesses, so resubmitting the same materials usually produces the same result.
In one recent similar case, a single line of name inconsistency triggered two rounds of supplementation.
Pinpointing what to fix for your specific situation requires reading the rejection notice itself carefully.

<h2 id="section-6">Stay Duration, Extensions, and Status Changes</h2>

H-2 has separate caps on per-grant stay length and on total cumulative stay.

### Managing Your Stay

The program is administered under the notices of the [Ministry of Justice Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr), and you need to file for an extension before your per-grant period expires.
Going even a single day over the deadline triggers a fine and creates disadvantages for any later change of status.

### Path to Switching to F-4

If you maintain legal employment on H-2 for a set period and meet specific conditions — such as work in agriculture/fishery/livestock, manufacturing, or non-metropolitan regions — the door to switching to F-4 opens.
The exact requirements shift each year, and the outcome depends on the combination of your occupation, work location, and length of continuous service.
Which conditions to chase to maximize your chances really comes down to case-by-case review.

<h2 id="section-7">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

**Q1. Can my family come with me on the H-2 visa?**
A spouse or minor children cannot enter as H-2 dependents.
They need to go through a separate Visiting Cohabitation (F-1) visa process, where the inviter's eligibility and income are reviewed independently.

**Q2. If I don't qualify for F-4, does that automatically mean H-2?**
Most of the time, yes — but it's worth re-checking your education, work history, and certifications, because F-4 can sometimes come back into play.
Before jumping straight to H-2, take one more pass at the F-4 requirements.

**Q3. Can I apply if I have a past record of unlawful stay in Korea?**
It depends on the case.
The outcome turns on whether you departed voluntarily, how much time has passed, and the severity of the violation, so individual review is essential.

**Q4. Do I have to take the Korean language test?**
If you're going through the computerized lottery rather than the relative-invitation track, the Korean language test is a prerequisite.
Test dates and validity periods change year to year, so check with the responsible agency.

**Q5. Can I change jobs after entering on H-2?**
Yes.
You just need to follow the procedures for reporting the start of work and any change of workplace — miss those and it becomes an issue at extension time.
