# Naturalization: General Naturalization Requirements and the Written Test — Starting With the Real Bottlenecks

General naturalization opens up only when all four pillars are cleared: five-plus years of lawful residence, the means-of-livelihood test, good conduct, and basic civic knowledge.

It applies to foreign nationals who don't have a Korean spouse or Korean lineage and want to acquire Korean nationality in their own name.

This article walks through the four pillars of general naturalization, how the written test (the Comprehensive Assessment for Naturalization) and interview are run, the points that most often trip applicants up in practice, and the order in which to prepare your documents.

<h2 id="section-1">The Four Pillars of General Naturalization — Where Most People Get Stuck</h2>

General naturalization is governed by Article 5 of the **Nationality Act**.

The statute reads simply enough, but the real dividing lines show up in practice.

The key isn't "five years of residence" — it's the *quality* of those five years.

### Five Years of Lawful Residence and the Domicile Requirement

As of the application date, you must have maintained a domicile in the Republic of Korea continuously for at least five years.

"Domicile" here doesn't just mean staying in the country — it means the period during which alien registration and actual residence were both maintained.

This is usually where things break down.

The most common failures involve a gap in visa status partway through, or cumulative time spent abroad long enough to break the "continuous" requirement.

You can't just take immigration records at face value when calculating time abroad — you have to review both the length of each individual trip and the cumulative total.

> **Caution:** Even short trips abroad, if they pile up, can break the five-year residence requirement. Check your own entry/exit records first at [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr).

### Can You Skip the Permanent Residency (F-5) Step?

F-5 permanent residency isn't a prerequisite for general naturalization.

That said, in actual review, applicants who have gone through the permanent residency stage tend to have their documentation evaluated more favorably.

Going directly without the permanent residency step is possible, but the scrutiny on income, taxes, and residence history gets noticeably tighter.

Whether that route works in your favor depends on a full review of your residence history.

<h2 id="section-2">The Means-of-Livelihood Requirement — Something Matters More Than the Paperwork</h2>

The first thing to look at is assets **held in your own name**.

The means-of-livelihood requirement is that you, or family members sharing a household with you, hold assets or income above a certain threshold.

The threshold amount is published annually and changes year to year, so confirm this year's exact figure during a consultation.

### What Counts as Qualifying Assets and Income

| Item | Recognized? | Notes |
|------|----------|------|
| Bank balance in your own name | Yes | Balance certificate + transaction history |
| Real estate in your own name | Yes | Based on registered copy and standard market value |
| Wage income | Yes | Withholding receipts, employment certificate |
| Business income | Yes | Comprehensive income tax filings |
| Assets in family members' names | Conditional | Must prove shared household |
| Short-term deposits | Weak | Falls apart if the source can't be explained |

In practice, reviewers weight *flow* more than *balance*.

Money sitting in your account doesn't help if the explanation of where it came from is shaky.

In particular, large deposits made right before applying almost always trigger a request for explanation.

### What if Your Tax History Is Weak?

Wage earners need consistent withholding records; self-employed applicants need a consistent comprehensive income tax filing history.

If filings are missing for certain years, or declared amounts are unrealistically low relative to revenue, the means test starts to wobble.

Tax delinquencies also tie into the good-conduct requirement, so don't treat them as a separate issue.

<h2 id="section-3">Good Conduct — It's Not Just About Criminal Record</h2>

The specifics of the good-conduct requirement are laid out in Article 5-2 of the **Enforcement Rules of the Nationality Act**.

You can view the text at the [Korean Law Information Center](https://www.law.go.kr).

People often assume "no criminal record is enough," but in practice the scope is broader.

### What Gets Reviewed

- Criminal history (including fines)
- Violations of the Immigration Control Act (overstays, failure to report, etc.)
- Tax arrears and unpaid National Health Insurance premiums
- Failure to meet family-law obligations such as unpaid child support
- Any prior residency permit obtained through improper means

A hit on any one of these will block your application even if you've cleared the residence requirement.

In particular, old immigration violations don't fade away just because time has passed.

> **Practical Tip:** It's safer to pull your own criminal record check before applying. If you go in without seeing the data yourself, you can end up suspended mid-review for reasons you didn't see coming.

<h2 id="section-4">Basic Civic Knowledge — The Comprehensive Assessment (Written Test) and Interview</h2>

The basic civic knowledge evaluation has two parts: the written **Comprehensive Assessment for Naturalization** and the **interview**.

Weakness here will sink your application no matter how solid the other requirements are.

### Test Format and Passing Score

| Section | Format | Items | Passing Threshold |
|------|------|------|--------|
| Comprehensive Assessment | Multiple choice + short answer | Set number of items | 60 or above |
| Interview | Oral | Korean language + civic awareness | Pass/Fail |

The comprehensive assessment covers the Korean language, Korean history, politics and economy, law and institutions, and customs.

You can find sample questions and patterns in materials from the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr) and the Korea Immigration & Integration Program (KIIP).

### When KIIP Completion Grants an Exemption

If you've completed KIIP through Stage 5 and passed its comprehensive assessment, the naturalization comprehensive assessment is waived.

Whether this exemption applies depends on when you completed the program and your assessment results — confirm before applying whether your completion level qualifies.

The interview is generally *not* exempt.

### What Actually Causes People to Fail the Interview

The interview isn't just basic Korean conversation.

You may be asked to recite part of the national anthem, the pledge to the flag, the administrative district where you live, and basic rights and duties.

Here's the key — they're not checking whether you memorized answers; they're checking whether you can describe your own life in Korean.

Precise word choice matters more than long answers.

<h2 id="section-5">The Application Process and Processing Time — How It Actually Moves</h2>

Processing times vary by Immigration Office.

### Process at a Glance

| Step | Content | Notes |
|------|------|------|
| 1 | Pre-application review of requirements | Residence, taxes, criminal history |
| 2 | File application at jurisdictional Immigration Office | In person or via representative |
| 3 | Comprehensive Assessment (written) | Confirm exemption eligibility |
| 4 | Interview | Korean language + civic awareness |
| 5 | Background check and field investigation | With cooperating agencies |
| 6 | Ministry of Justice review and Nationality Deliberation Committee | Final decision |
| 7 | Public notice of nationality acquisition and citizen's oath | Renounce foreign nationality within 1 year |

The total timeline runs anywhere from the late one-year range to two or three years.

Processing times vary by office, and the fastest route for your specific case is something we'll map out on a case-by-case basis.

### Renouncing Foreign Nationality and the Non-Exercise Pledge

Within one year of the nationality acquisition decision, you must either renounce your foreign nationality or submit a Pledge to Not Exercise Foreign Nationality.

The non-exercise pledge is only available to those who meet the eligibility conditions.

This area sits at the intersection of your home country's nationality law and Korean nationality law, so the procedure splits depending on your original nationality.

<h2 id="section-6">Common Stumbling Points and How to Shore Them Up</h2>

In recent comparable cases, the three most common reasons for suspension have been:

### Case 1 — Five Years Met, But Long Cumulative Time Abroad

Even with five years of stay, if your cumulative one-year time abroad runs long, "continuous residence" breaks.

You need to pull your entry/exit records before applying and calculate them day by day.

### Case 2 — Means Test Met, But Weak Source-of-Funds Explanation

Submitting only a balance certificate and skipping transaction history or income documents triggers a follow-up request.

Even with a stack of documents, a weak fund-flow narrative can derail things quickly.

### Case 3 — Old Immigration Violations Treated as Minor at the Time

Old overstays or failures to report come back to life during the good-conduct review.

The most common pattern we see is records the applicant had forgotten about resurfacing mid-review.

<h2 id="section-7">FAQ</h2>

**Q1. Can I apply for general naturalization directly without permanent residency (F-5)?**

Yes.

Permanent residency isn't a prerequisite.

That said, because you haven't gone through the document scrutiny at the PR stage, the verification of income and residence history runs tighter.

**Q2. If I complete the Korea Immigration & Integration Program (KIIP), are both the test and the interview waived?**

The comprehensive assessment may be waived, but the interview is usually still conducted separately.

Application depends on your completion level and timing, so confirm in advance.

**Q3. Can I retake the written test if I fail?**

Yes, retakes are allowed.

That said, retake schedules and limits vary based on operational guidelines, so the earliest available retake date after your failure is something to confirm with the relevant office.

**Q4. Where do I check the threshold for the means-of-livelihood requirement?**

The threshold is published annually and changes year to year.

You can find it in announcements from the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr) and in Ministry of Justice notices, and the figure that applies to you varies by household composition.

**Q5. I have a Korean spouse — should I still go through general naturalization?**

If you have a Korean spouse, the simplified naturalization route generally comes first.

That said, applicants who haven't met the marriage-duration or in-country residence requirements sometimes go through general naturalization, so reviewing your specific situation is the starting point.

**Q6. What happens if I leave the country mid-application?**

Extended absence during review affects whether you continue to meet the residence requirement.

It can also conflict with interview and background-check schedules, so it's safer to flag any travel plans in advance and coordinate.
