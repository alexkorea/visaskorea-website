# Complete Guide to E-9 Visa Workplace Change Procedures and Requirements

Changing workplaces on an E-9 visa is only permitted with employer consent or for legally recognized reasons, and if a new workplace isn't secured within three months of filing, the worker becomes subject to departure from Korea.

This applies to foreign workers who entered Korea under the Non-Professional Employment (E-9) status in manufacturing, agriculture/livestock, fisheries, construction, or services, and the change must fall under the grounds set forth in Article 25 of the Act on the Employment of Foreign Workers.

This guide covers the legal grounds, application procedures, frequency limits, and the practical pitfalls that trip workers up most often — all in one place.

<h2 id="section-1">E-9 Workplace Change: What to Know First</h2>

E-9 status does not give you the freedom to switch employers at will.

A workplace change is only allowed when one of the grounds set out in Article 25 of the Act on the Employment of Foreign Workers (the Foreign Employment Act) applies.

The key distinction is between **a standard change made with employer consent** and **a change triggered by the employer's fault.**

These two paths differ entirely in required documents, processing steps, and how the change is counted against your limit.

### Legal Basis for E-9 Workplace Change

The governing provisions are [Article 25 of the Act on the Employment of Foreign Workers](https://www.law.go.kr) and Article 30 of its Enforcement Decree.

The Employment Permit System is operated by the [Ministry of Employment and Labor](https://www.moel.go.kr) and the [HRD-Net Employment Permit System portal](https://www.eps.go.kr).

Changes to status of stay are processed through [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) and your local Immigration Office.

> **Note:** The Foreign Employment Act is amended frequently. Whether your specific grounds meet the current standard should always be confirmed with the relevant authority.

### Permitted vs. Not Permitted at a Glance

| Category | Permitted Grounds | Not Permitted |
|------|---------------|----------------|
| Employer Consent | Mutual agreement + contract termination | Simple dissatisfaction with pay |
| Closure / Suspension | Business suspension, closure, or insolvency | Temporary halt in operations |
| Employer's Fault | Unpaid wages, verbal/physical abuse, sexual harassment | Dismissal due to the worker's fault |
| Working Conditions | Industrial accident, unsafe workplace | Mere difficulty adjusting |
| Employment Permit | Cancellation/restriction of employer's permit | Worker's unauthorized absence |

<h2 id="section-2">Grounds Recognized for Workplace Change</h2>

Grounds for an E-9 workplace change fall into four broad categories.

Each category requires different evidence and is weighed differently in practice, so the first step is identifying exactly which one your situation fits.

### Change with Employer Consent

The cleanest path is when the employer agrees to terminate the labor contract.

In this case, the employer files the workplace change directly through the [EPS (Employment Permit System) portal](https://www.eps.go.kr).

In practice, it's common for employers to hand over a written consent but then delay the EPS filing.

When this happens, the entire process stalls — and the worker is the only one who pays the price.

### Employer's Fault

Where the employer is at fault — unpaid wages, verbal or physical abuse, sexual harassment, forced labor, suspension or closure — the change can be made without the worker's consent.

But this is where things get complicated.

Unpaid wages must be substantiated through a formal complaint filed with the [Korea Workers' Compensation & Welfare Service](https://www.comwel.or.kr) or your local Regional Labor Office.

Simply walking in and asserting "there were unpaid wages" will not be accepted.

### Breach of Working Conditions, Industrial Accidents, Work Environment

Being forced to perform duties different from those in the labor contract, suffering an industrial accident, or facing safety hazards in the workplace are all recognized grounds.

For industrial accidents, the outcome of an [industrial accident claim filed with the Korea Workers' Compensation & Welfare Service](https://www.comwel.or.kr) is typically the decisive piece of evidence.

> **Practical tip:** Half of proving your grounds is having a record from when it happened. Pay stubs, KakaoTalk messages, audio recordings, medical certificates — gathering these contemporaneously is what keeps you from losing ground during the actual review.

### Grounds Caused by the Worker Are Not Recognized

Unauthorized absences, assaulting the employer, or dismissals attributable to the worker are not accepted as grounds for change.

In fact, these can put your visa status itself at risk, which makes them particularly dangerous.

<h2 id="section-3">Workplace Change Application Procedure</h2>

An E-9 workplace change moves through four stages: filing with the Employment Center → job placement → labor contract with the new employer → immigration notification.

Each stage has its own deadline, and missing even one by a day can derail the whole process.

### Step 1: File the Workplace Change

Once a reason for terminating the labor contract arises, you must file a workplace change application with your local [Employment Center](https://www.work.go.kr) within one month of the triggering event.

For employer-consent cases, the employer files; for employer-fault cases, the worker can file directly.

The filing date must be on record for the job-search period that follows to be granted properly.

### Step 2: Three-Month Job Search Period

Once the application is accepted, you are registered as a job seeker in the EPS system and have three months to find a new workplace.

If you do not sign a labor contract with a new employer within three months, you become subject to departure.

That said, the period may be extended in cases of work-related injury, illness, pregnancy or childbirth, or where the change was filed for reasons not attributable to the worker.

Whether an extension is granted varies case by case, so checking in advance is essential.

### Step 3: Job Placement and Signing the New Contract

Once you are matched with a new employer through the EPS system, you sign the standard labor contract and the new employer obtains an employment permit from the [Employment Center](https://www.work.go.kr).

This is fundamentally different from standard hiring, where you would freely pick a company, interview, and start work on your own.

For E-9 holders, private hiring outside the EPS process is not recognized at all.

### Step 4: Notify Immigration

Once you begin working at the new workplace, you must file a workplace change notification with your local Immigration Office within 14 days.

This can be done online through [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) or in person.

Failing to file this notification triggers an administrative fine.

| Step | Deadline | Filed By | Notes |
|------|------|------|------|
| Workplace change application | Within 1 month of the triggering event | Employer or worker | Employment Center |
| Job search period | 3 months from the filing date | Worker | EPS registration |
| Labor contract & employment permit | Within the job search period | New employer | Standard contract |
| Workplace change notification | Within 14 days of starting work | Worker | Immigration filing |

<h2 id="section-4">Limits on Number of Workplace Changes</h2>

E-9 status caps the number of workplace changes you can make during your stay.

Exceeding this cap effectively ends your ability to keep working in Korea.

### General Rule: Three Times, Plus Extra on Re-employment

As a rule, you are allowed up to three workplace changes during the initial three-year stay following entry.

If you are re-employed for an additional one year and ten months, you may make up to two more changes during that extension period.

However, changes attributable to the employer's fault do not count toward this cap.

This is where the difference shows up.

Even for the same "job change," whether it counts against your cap depends entirely on how the grounds are classified.

### Cases Excluded from the Count

Changes objectively shown to result from the employer's fault — closure, unpaid wages, assault, sexual harassment, unfair treatment — are excluded from the count.

The catch is that "objective proof" is held to a very strict standard in the actual review.

If you have only filed a complaint and no outcome has been issued, your case may be classified as a simple "consent-based change" and counted against your cap.

> **Practical tip:** You don't decide how your grounds are classified — the Employment Center and Labor Office do. The way to keep a change from counting against your cap is to map out the evidence before you file, not after.

<h2 id="section-5">Where People Actually Get Stuck</h2>

On paper the process looks simple, but in real workplace-change cases the failure points are very specific.

### 1) Missing the 1-Month or 3-Month Deadline

If you fail to file the workplace change within one month of the triggering event, the procedure is effectively shut down.

If you exceed the three-month job-search period, you become subject to departure.

This happens most often to workers who aren't familiar with Korean administrative procedures and end up trusting the employer to handle it, only to miss the deadline waiting.

### 2) Insufficient Evidence of Grounds

Statements alone — "the boss cursed at me," "wages were late" — will not get your case classified as employer's fault.

You need objective records: recordings, text messages, pay stubs, medical certificates, the Labor Office complaint receipt.

If this is weak, the case will be processed as if you quit for personal reasons, and the change will count against your cap.

### 3) Attempting Private Hiring

Even if you find a new workplace yourself and reach an agreement directly with the employer, no employment permit will be issued unless you go through EPS job placement.

Starting work in this state constitutes illegal employment and makes you subject to forced departure.

### 4) Missing the Workplace Change Notification

Starting work at the new place isn't the end of it.

If you fail to file the workplace change notification with Immigration within 14 days, an administrative fine will be imposed.

This omission can also weigh against you in stay-extension or re-employment reviews.

### 5) Mistiming a Re-employment or E-7-4 Transition

Attempting a workplace change near the end of your E-9 stay tends to collide with the schedule for re-employment or [transition to E-7-4 (Skilled Worker) status](https://www.hikorea.go.kr).

Transition points and tenure requirements can collapse, so running the timing through a simulation in advance comes first.

> **Note:** The E-7-4 scoring categories and thresholds are adjusted every year. To avoid losing ground, you have to set your workplace-change timing based on where your score actually stands.

<h2 id="section-7">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

**Q1. If my employer agrees, can I switch companies freely?**

Even with employer consent, the EPS system registration and Employment Center procedure are mandatory.

A privately arranged move is not recognized.

**Q2. I quit because wages were unpaid — does that get excluded from my count?**

The unpaid wages have to be objectively documented.

You'll need the outcome of a complaint filed with your [Regional Labor Office](https://www.moel.go.kr) or a confirmation of unpaid amounts to have the case classified as employer's fault.

Whether your specific situation qualifies requires a case-by-case review.

**Q3. If I can't find a new job within three months, do I have to leave the country immediately?**

In principle yes, but the period can be extended for reasons not attributable to you, or for illness, industrial accidents, and similar circumstances.

Whether an extension is granted needs to be confirmed with the relevant authority.

**Q4. I'm planning to transition from E-9 to E-7-4 (Skilled Worker) — does changing workplaces hurt me?**

Yes, it can affect categories like tenure points and the "same-workplace" bonus.

You need to recalculate whether and when to change based on your transition timing to avoid losing ground.

**Q5. After filing the workplace change, can I take time off?**

In principle, you cannot work until a new employment permit is issued.

Working temporarily at another company in the meantime constitutes illegal employment.

**Q6. What if my employer keeps delaying the EPS filing?**

There is a path for the worker to file directly by demonstrating the employer's fault.

If the supporting evidence is weak at that point, the case will be processed as a standard consent-based change and count against your cap, so preparation up front is the key.
