# F-1-12 Dependent Visa for Foreign Children: Where Applications Most Often Go Wrong in Practice

The F-1-12 is a dependent status granted to the minor children of foreign parents who are legally staying in Korea on a long-term basis.

It applies when a parent holds a qualifying D, E, or F-series status, and the child is under 19 and lives with the parent.

This article walks through who is eligible, how eligibility differs by parent status, what documents are required, how the application process works, the practical points where things most often get stuck, and what to watch for on extension or status changes.

<h2 id="section-1">Defining F-1-12 and Who It Covers</h2>

F-1-12 is not simply a "child visa." It is a dependent status that derives from the parent's status of stay.

The key point is that if the parent's status becomes unstable, the child's F-1-12 becomes unstable along with it.

### Who Can Apply

The status generally covers children who meet all of the following conditions:

- At least one parent holds a valid long-term status in Korea (such as D-2, D-7, D-8, D-10, E-1 through E-7, F-2, F-4, etc.)

- The child is a minor under 19 years of age

- The child will live with the parent and be raised in Korea

It looks straightforward on paper, but in practice whether the dependent status is allowed depends heavily on the type of parent status.

In particular, non-professional or unskilled-labor categories such as E-9, E-10, and H-2 generally do not allow dependents, and whether your specific situation qualifies should be confirmed through consultation.

### How It Differs From F-3

F-3 dependent status and F-1-12 are often confused.

F-3 is the dependent status granted to spouses and minor children of holders of certain D and E categories, while F-1-12 is a separate sub-code issued within the F-1 framework.

Whether a child receives F-3 or F-1-12 depends on the parent's status, and if this distinction is unclear from the start, the wrong application form gets filed.

<h2 id="section-2">Whether Children Can Accompany You, by Parent Status</h2>

The first thing to check is whether the parent's status of stay actually permits dependents.

It is genuinely common to have a valid status that nonetheless does not allow children to accompany you.

### Status-by-Status Reference Table

| Parent Status | Dependents Allowed? | Typical Child Status Code |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D-2 Study | Yes | F-3 |
| D-7 / D-8 / D-9 | Yes | F-3 |
| D-10 Job Seeker | Restricted | Case-by-case review |
| E-1 to E-7 | Yes | F-3 |
| E-9 / E-10 | Generally not permitted | Case-by-case |
| F-2 Resident | Yes | F-1-12 or F-2 dependent |
| F-4 Overseas Korean | Yes | F-1-12 |
| F-5 Permanent Resident | Yes | F-2-2, etc. |

The table reflects general standards. Actual outcomes vary depending on the parent's remaining stay period, income, and the child's age.

### Parent-Status Scenarios That Often Get Stuck

This is usually where applications stall.

If the parent is on D-10, or entered on F-4 but cannot strongly demonstrate stable income, an F-1-12 application for the child does not sail through.

If the parent's remaining stay is under six months, or an extension is coming up soon, you need to decide carefully whether to file the child's application first or the parent's extension first.

Getting that sequence wrong means the child's application sits pending, or gets shaken loose again when the parent's status changes.

<h2 id="section-3">Documents Required for an F-1-12 Application</h2>

More important than the paperwork itself is proving the family relationship and explaining the parent's ability to support and raise the child.

Even a thick stack of documents will trigger requests for additional materials if the family relationship looks weak.

### Common Required Documents

- Integrated application form (Form No. 34)

- Copy of the child's passport and standard-format photo

- Copies of the parents' Alien Registration Cards and passports

- Documents proving family relationship (birth certificate, family register, family relations certificate, etc.)

- Documents proving the child's place of residence in Korea (housing lease, family resident registration, etc.)

- Proof of the parents' employment and income (employment certificate, business registration, income certificate, etc.)

- Child's academic records, if applicable: enrollment certificate, admission letter, etc.

### The Trap With Documents Issued in the Home Country

Birth certificates and family relations documents issued abroad are not the end of the story on their own.

You will additionally need an apostille from the issuing country or consular confirmation by the Korean consulate, plus a Korean translation that includes the translator's identifying information.

> **Note:** Family-relations documents issued in the home country may be rejected if they were issued too long ago. Prepare recently issued versions.

If the translation is done by the applicant, some immigration offices have rejected it, so it is safer to submit the translator's credentials along with the document.

<h2 id="section-4">Application Process and Flow</h2>

The procedure for F-1-12 differs depending on whether the child is still abroad or already in Korea.

### Applying From Abroad

If the child is in their home country, the usual route is for the parent in Korea to apply for a Certificate of Visa Issuance, and the child then uses that confirmation number to receive the visa at the Korean embassy in the home country.

| Step | Where It Happens | Key Point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Apply for Certificate of Visa Issuance | Korean Immigration Office | Parent applies; family relationship and status must be proven |
| 2. Certificate issued | Korean Immigration | Usually 2–4 weeks |
| 3. Visa issued | Korean Embassy in home country | Child applies using the confirmation number |
| 4. Alien registration after entry | Local immigration office | Within 90 days of arrival |

Processing times vary by office, and finding the fastest jurisdiction is the starting point for practical case management.

### Applying as a Status Change From Within Korea

If the child has already entered Korea on a short-term visit (C-3) or similar status, in some cases you can apply for a status change to F-1-12 from within Korea without leaving the country.

That said, if the purpose of entry does not align with the rationale for the status change, the application can be denied outright, so this needs to be planned before the child arrives.

<h2 id="section-6">Points That Frequently Trip Up the Actual Review</h2>

Before worrying about approval rates, the first question to answer is: "Why does this child need to come to Korea at this specific time?"

The key insight is that reviewers care less about the volume of paperwork and more about whether the dependency is reasonable and whether you can actually raise the child here.

### Caregiving Capacity and Housing

Even when the parent's income is solid, reviewers commonly request supplemental information if the housing is not appropriate for a child.

Single-occupancy formats like one-room studios or gosiwons can look weak as proof that a minor child will actually live with the parent.

If housing proof is thin, the review drags out no matter how clean the family-relations paperwork is.

### Consistency in Proving the Family Relationship

If the names on the birth certificate, on the parents' passports, and on their Korean alien registration records do not match exactly, the case gets tangled immediately.

Reconciling differences in alphabet spelling, hanja characters, and date-of-birth formats is the very first thing to check in practice.

When this is not properly explained, the resulting requests for additional documents can extend processing time by a month or more.

### The Parent's Remaining Period of Stay

If you apply for a child dependent when the parent's stay period is nearly expired, the child's permission will also be granted only for a short period matching the parent's.

That puts you in a position of having to apply for an extension shortly after the child's arrival, so timing the parent's renewal and the child's application together is where efficiency is won or lost.

<h2 id="section-7">Considerations for Extending or Changing Status</h2>

Because F-1-12 derives from the parent's status, any change in the parent's status requires reviewing the child's status as well.

### When the Parent Converts to F-5

If the parent converts to permanent residence (F-5), in some cases the child needs to be re-aligned from F-1-12 to F-2-2 or a similar status.

Missing this transition point can destabilize the child's stay and complicate how residence time is counted later when applying for permanent residence or citizenship.

### When the Child Turns 19

F-1-12 is premised on the holder being a minor, so once the child reaches adulthood they need to transition to an independent status of their own (D-2 study, D-10 job seeker, etc.).

The options narrow as the transition point approaches, depending on the child's academic and career situation, so it is safest to start planning the status transition six months before they turn 19.

Operational standards around transition timing have changed recently in places, so whether the rules apply to your specific situation should be confirmed by a professional.

### Re-Entry and Alien Registration

If the child does not complete alien registration within 90 days of entering Korea, they become subject to a fine.

Also, re-entry permission is waived in some cases for re-entries within one year of departure, but if you exceed one year, the status of stay can automatically lapse.

The relevant legal basis is in the [Immigration Control Act](https://www.law.go.kr) and its enforcement decree.

<h2 id="section-8">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

### Q1. If only one parent is staying in Korea and the other parent is in the home country, can the child still get F-1-12?

Yes.

You will, however, need to explain how the parent in Korea can raise the child on their own, and reviewers frequently ask for additional materials such as a consent letter from the parent in the home country.

### Q2. Can a parent on D-10 (job seeker) bring their child to Korea?

D-10 is, in principle, a status that does not allow dependents.

It is generally safer in practice to convert to an employment status such as E-7 and then apply, and the right timing for your specific situation should be reviewed through consultation.

### Q3. Is F-1-12 difficult to obtain if the child will not attend school in Korea?

School enrollment is not an absolute requirement.

That said, if a child aged 7 or older is not attending school and the reason is not well explained, the review can drag on, so it is safer to prepare materials showing future enrollment plans.

### Q4. The birth certificate issued in our home country is very old. Do we need to obtain a new one?

If possible, it is safer to obtain a freshly issued copy.

Older documents can be difficult to apostille in the first place, and some immigration offices require copies issued within the last six months.

### Q5. Can a child on F-1-12 work part-time in Korea?

F-1-12 is, in principle, a status that does not permit employment.

That said, in certain conditions (such as the child being 18 or older), limited allowances like part-time employment may be possible, and the exact scope needs to be confirmed with the local immigration office.

### Q6. How is health insurance handled for a child on F-1-12 in Korea?

If a parent is a workplace subscriber, the child can in some cases be registered as a dependent; otherwise, the typical structure is for the child to enroll individually as a regional subscriber.

Detailed enrollment procedures can be found at the [National Health Insurance Service](https://www.nhis.or.kr).
