# Visa Refusal Appeals and Administrative Litigation: A Practical Procedural Guide

A visa refusal does not mean the road ends there.

The foreign national who received the refusal notice — or the inviting party in Korea — can choose among an objection (administrative reconsideration), an administrative appeal, or administrative litigation, depending on which procedure best fits the specific case.

This guide covers what you should look at first immediately after a refusal, the step-by-step procedure, and the points where people most often get stuck in practice.

<h2 id="section-1">What to Check First When You Receive a Visa Refusal Notice</h2>

Many people jump straight to thinking about reapplying the moment they receive a refusal notice, but if you get the order wrong, you will be refused again on the very same ground.

What you need to look at first is the exact wording of the refusal reason and the specific legal provision applied.

### Why You Must Read the Refusal Notice Word for Word

Phrases like "insufficient documents submitted," "purpose of stay unclear," or "inadequate grounds for invitation" each mean very different things in practice.

They look similar on the surface, but some grounds can be resolved simply by supplementing your documents, while others will block you permanently unless you contest the disposition itself.

In particular, if you have been notified that your case falls under the entry-prohibition grounds in Article 11 of the Immigration Act, a simple reapplication will not unlock anything.

Keep the original refusal notice as a PDF — not a photograph — and be sure to record the date of notification.

### The Notification Date Is the Starting Point for Every Deadline

The deadlines for objections, administrative appeals, and administrative litigation are all calculated from the day you received the notice.

This is usually where people stumble.

If you spend too long agonizing over the refusal and miss the deadline, the most powerful avenue — administrative litigation — closes off entirely.

<h2 id="section-2">The Differences Between Objections, Administrative Appeals, and Administrative Litigation</h2>

Many people mistakenly treat these three procedures as if they were the same thing.

| Type | Filed With | Filing Deadline | Characteristics |
|------|----------|----------|------|
| Objection | The issuing authority (Immigration Office Director) | A short window from the notification date | Informal, simplified procedure; same agency re-reviews |
| Administrative Appeal | Central Administrative Appeals Commission | 90 days from knowing of the disposition; 180 days from the date it was issued | Internal administrative control procedure |
| Administrative Litigation | Competent Administrative Court | 90 days from knowing of the disposition; 1 year from the date it was issued | Court rules on the legality of the disposition |

### The Objection Is the Fastest but Weakest Route

An objection is a procedure for asking the very Immigration Office that issued the refusal to review the case once more.

Its strengths are speed and minimal cost.

But this is also where the problem starts.

Because the same agency is looking at the same materials again, the chances of the outcome being reversed are low unless you can add supplementary documents or new facts.

In practice, when the refusal reason is something that can be supplemented — such as "insufficient documents" — there are cases where submitting additional materials at the objection stage resolves the matter.

### Administrative Appeals Are Reviewed by the Central Administrative Appeals Commission

In an administrative appeal, the decision is made not by the issuing authority but by the [Central Administrative Appeals Commission](https://www.simpan.go.kr).

This brings more objectivity than an objection, and representation by attorneys or administrative agents (haengjeongsa) is allowed.

That said, in an administrative appeal the burden of proving the facts lies with the petitioner, and the heart of the case is the submission of new evidence and disputes over how the law should be interpreted.

### Administrative Litigation Is the Last Card

In administrative litigation, the court directly determines whether the disposition was unlawful.

If you win, the disposition is annulled, and immigration authorities must reissue a decision consistent with the court's reasoning.

The cost and duration are the highest, but the heavier the disposition — such as entry prohibition or deportation — the more administrative litigation becomes effectively the only viable path.

<h2 id="section-3">Step-by-Step Procedure and Practical Workflow</h2>

### Step 1 — Analyzing the Refusal Reason

Gather the refusal notice, the documents originally submitted with your application, and a copy of the application form in one place.

Whether the refusal reason is "insufficient financial capacity," "unclear purpose of stay," or "subject to entry prohibition" completely changes what your next step should be.

### Step 2 — Choosing the Procedure

The three procedures can run in parallel, but typically the order is: objection → administrative appeal → administrative litigation.

That said, if the refusal clearly comes down to a question of legal interpretation, skipping the objection and going straight to an administrative appeal or litigation saves time.

> **Caution:** The deadline for filing administrative litigation (90 days from learning of the disposition) does not automatically extend just because you filed an objection. Miscalculating this deadline and losing the right to sue altogether is one of the most common mistakes seen in practice.

### Step 3 — Strengthening the Evidence

To reverse a refusal, you need to produce and submit facts or materials that did not exist or were lacking at the time of the original disposition.

Having a lot of documents means nothing if they are unrelated to the refusal reason.

The key is to build rebuttal materials that precisely address the one line stating the refusal reason.

<h2 id="section-4">How the Practical Response Diverges Depending on the Refusal Reason</h2>

### Insufficient Documents or Unmet Requirements

This is the type of refusal that is most often resolved through supplementary materials.

The fastest route is usually to fill in exactly what was flagged — proof of income, business performance, academic credentials, and so on — and file either an objection or a new application.

### Unclear Purpose of Stay or Doubts About Authenticity

This is when your documents are in order, but the officer doubts your motivation.

If this concern is weak, resubmitting the same documents will not change anything.

What needs to be redone are the "explanatory documents" — the business plan, activity plan, and the inviter's written statement.

### Entry Prohibition or Prior Immigration Violations

A notice stating that you fall under the entry-prohibition grounds in Article 11 of the Immigration Act is the heaviest type of refusal.

Unless you directly contest the legality of the disposition through an administrative appeal or litigation, entering Korea will be effectively impossible.

In a recent similar case, a refusal based on a prior immigration violation history was overturned at the administrative appeal stage.

However, the supporting evidence and circumstances differ from case to case, so whether the same outcome is possible in your case requires separate review.

> **Practical tip:** If two or more reasons are stated in the refusal, you must tackle the heaviest one first. Rebutting only the lighter reason leaves the heavier one intact, and you will be refused again.

<h2 id="section-5">How Administrative Litigation Actually Unfolds</h2>

### Drafting and Filing the Complaint

Administrative litigation begins by naming the issuing authority as the defendant and filing a complaint with the competent Administrative Court.

The complaint must state the content of the disposition, the date of notification, the grounds of illegality, and the relief sought.

Under Article 20 of the Administrative Litigation Act, the filing deadline is 90 days from learning of the disposition and 1 year from the date the disposition was issued.

### Exchange of Answer and Briefs

Once the defendant (the Immigration Office) files its answer, the two sides exchange written briefs, contesting the facts and the interpretation of the law.

This stage is often where the "precise meaning" of the refusal reason first appears spelled out in a formal document.

### Oral Hearing and Judgment

At the oral hearing, both sides organize their arguments, and the court issues its judgment.

If you win, the disposition is annulled, and the immigration authorities must reissue a decision in line with the judgment.

### Processing Time and Cost

Processing time varies depending on the court and the complexity of the case, but a first-instance decision typically takes several months or more.

Costs differ case by case, and we provide a precise estimate during the free consultation.

| Procedure | Average Processing Time | Effect of Outcome |
|------|--------------|----------|
| Objection | Relatively short (varies by office) | Re-review by the issuing authority |
| Administrative Appeal | Typically several months | If granted, disposition is annulled |
| Administrative Litigation (1st instance) | Several months or more | Upon final judgment, disposition is annulled |

Exact processing times vary by immigration office and by court, so the schedule for your particular case requires separate confirmation.

<h2 id="section-6">Practical Points People Often Miss</h2>

### Do Not File a Reapplication and an Objection at the Same Time

If you file a new application first and it is refused, the new refusal reason is added on top of the old one, broadening the scope of dispute in any subsequent administrative appeal or litigation.

It is cleaner to first assess the nature of the refusal reason, then choose only one of reapplication, objection, or administrative appeal.

### The Inviter's Cooperation Documents Are Decisive

When an invitation-based visa is refused, additional statements from the inviter, financial documentation, and business performance records often turn out to be the key to reversing the refusal.

Trying to fight the case with the applicant's documents alone has clear limits.

### Accuracy of Interpretation and Translation Materials

Documents issued abroad and foreign-language evidence must go through notarization and apostille procedures to be accepted as evidence in administrative appeals and litigation.

If this part is weak, large volumes of material will not be recognized.

For detailed procedures, you can review the original text of the Immigration Act and the Administrative Litigation Act directly at the [Korean Law Information Center](https://www.law.go.kr).

Official guidance on visa issuance is updated at [HiKorea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr) and the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr).

<h2 id="section-7">Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)</h2>

**Q1. Can I just file a new visa application immediately after a refusal?**

If the original ground for refusal still applies, a new application will simply be refused again.

You first need to analyze the refusal reason and decide whether it is something that can be cured with supplementary documents or whether the disposition itself must be contested.

**Q2. Can I file an objection and an administrative appeal at the same time?**

It is allowed by the system, but in practice contesting the same issue in two forums at once is not efficient.

Choosing one procedure first, based on the nature of the refusal reason, tends to produce better results.

**Q3. If I win the administrative litigation, will the visa be issued right away?**

A favorable judgment has the effect of annulling the refusal, after which the immigration authorities reissue a decision consistent with the ruling.

If a new disqualifying ground is discovered, you may still be refused again.

**Q4. Can entry-prohibition orders also be contested through administrative litigation?**

Yes, they can.

However, when the entry-prohibition ground is combined with a criminal conviction or a prior deportation, the evidentiary burden grows substantially, so case-by-case review is necessary.

**Q5. If I lose at the administrative appeal stage, can I still go to administrative litigation?**

Yes.

You can file administrative litigation within 90 days of receiving the administrative appeal ruling.

**Q6. Can I pursue administrative litigation by myself, without a lawyer?**

Legally, yes.

But because the case turns on legal interpretation of the refusal grounds and on how the evidence is structured, there are many parts that are genuinely difficult to handle alone in practice.
