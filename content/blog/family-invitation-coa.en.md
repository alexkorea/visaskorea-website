# Issuing a Certificate of Visa Issuance for Inviting Foreign Family Members: Starting From the Real Sticking Points

Issuing a Certificate of Visa Issuance for inviting foreign family members works like this: the inviter in Korea applies in advance at the Immigration & Foreigner Office to obtain the certificate, and the family member then uses that certificate number to receive a visa at the Korean diplomatic mission in their home country.

The typical candidates are F-2, F-5, or F-6 holders, permanent residents, and some long-term foreign residents inviting a spouse, minor child, or parent from their home country. The procedure differs from F-3, which is for accompanying dependents.

Below, we walk through eligibility, document preparation, the points where reviews diverge, and how to respond after a rejection — in the order things actually unfold in practice.

<h2 id="section-1">Why the Certificate Route Exists Separately</h2>

### How It Differs From Applying Directly at a Consulate Abroad

Family invitation cases can also be filed directly at the Korean diplomatic mission in the home country, but when the inviter is in Korea and the dependency relationship and income must be proven with Korean-issued documents, the certificate route is usually faster.

Direct consulate applications require the applicant to gather and submit everything locally, which means Korean-side documents have to be sent over piece by piece.

The certificate route, by contrast, completes the review in one place at Korean Immigration, and the home country only needs to issue the visa against the certificate number.

### Which Statuses Benefit Most From the Certificate Route

Typical cases include permanent residents (F-5) or marriage migrants (F-6) inviting parents or children from their home country, and overseas Koreans (F-4) inviting direct relatives.

Work or investment status holders such as D-7, D-8, or E-7 who bring family on F-3 dependent status also frequently use the certificate route.

> **Practical tip:** If the family relationship documents were issued in the home country, get an apostille or consular confirmation in advance — otherwise the review will stall.

<h2 id="section-2">Inviter Eligibility: The First Thing to Check</h2>

### Scope of Invitation by Residence Status

The range of family members an inviter can bring depends on the visa they hold.

| Inviter Residence Status | Eligible Family Members | Resulting Visa |
|---|---|---|
| F-5 Permanent Resident | Spouse, minor children, some parents | F-2, F-1, etc. |
| F-6 Marriage Migrant | Direct relatives such as parents in home country | F-1 |
| F-4 Overseas Korean | Spouse, lineal ascendants/descendants | F-1 |
| D-7, D-8, D-10, E-1–E-7 | Spouse, minor children | F-3 |
| Student (D-2) | Spouse, minor children | F-3 |

The table shows the broad strokes. In practice, parent invitations also factor in age, whether you live together, and whether other supporters exist back home.

If it's unclear which row your case fits into, sort that out at the consultation stage before anything else.

### Financial Capacity: Don't Read It as Just a Number

Income and assets are evaluated on flow and stability rather than the bare amount.

Steady wage income can still look weak if the employer changes often, and self-employment with strong revenue still trips up if continuity-of-business documents are missing.

Financial capacity thresholds are updated annually by official notice, so the exact figure for your household size this year needs to be checked separately against the [Korea Immigration Service](https://www.immigration.go.kr) announcement.

<h2 id="section-3">Proving the Family Relationship: This Is Where Cases Split</h2>

### Handling Home-Country Documents

To prove a spousal, child, or parental relationship, the official documents issued by the home country government must be put into a form Korea will accept.

For apostille convention member countries, it's a single apostille step; for non-member countries, it's a two-step process — authentication by the home country's foreign ministry followed by consular confirmation at the local Korean embassy.

Miss the validity window between the issue date and arrival in Korea and you'll have to reissue everything.

### Notarized Translation and Name Consistency

If the name on a home-country document differs from the passport, that alone becomes grounds for a supplemental request.

This is especially common with Chinese, Russian, and Central Asian documents, where the same person's name often appears differently across documents, so an identity-confirmation certificate has to be submitted alongside.

> **Caution:** Notarized translations must be processed through a notary office — they aren't simple translations, and your own translation will not be accepted.

<h2 id="section-4">The Application Process and Workflow</h2>

### Step-by-Step Flow

| Step | Applicant | Place / Method | Key Point |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Document preparation | Inviter + invitee | Korea and home country simultaneously | Home-country documents authenticated first |
| 2. Certificate application | Inviter | Local immigration office or Hi Korea | Evidence of dependency relationship |
| 3. Review and issuance | Immigration | Korea | Certificate number notified |
| 4. Visa application | Invitee | Korean mission in home country | Certificate number + passport |
| 5. Entry and alien registration | Invitee | Korea | Register within 90 days |

Applications can also be filed electronically through [Hi Korea](https://www.hikorea.go.kr), but for matters that require review of original family-relationship documents, in-person filing makes supplemental requests easier to handle.

### Processing Time

Processing time varies widely between offices and shifts with the family composition, nationality, and time of year.

Choosing the fastest jurisdiction and timing depends on the specifics of each case, so confirm the right path for yours through a consultation.

<h2 id="section-5">Where Reviews Actually Diverge</h2>

### The "Substance" of the Dependency Relationship

Even when the family tie is documented, the review weakens if there's no visible intent or capacity to actually live together.

For parent invitations, reviewers also look at whether other supporters remain in the home country and whether siblings are already providing support there.

If this isn't explained well, supplemental requests drag on no matter how many documents you submit.

### How Settled the Inviter Is in Korea

How stably the inviter is living in Korea is also weighed.

This includes housing type, length of residence at the same address, continuity of employment or business, and any past immigration violations.

In a recent similar case, income was sufficient but frequent address changes within a year were flagged as grounds for a supplemental request.

### Past Visa History

The invitee's own past entry history into Korea is reflected directly.

Records of entering on a short-term visit (C-3) and attempting long-term stay, prior rejections, or past overstays all surface first at the certificate stage.

<h2 id="section-6">Responding to Rejection or Supplemental Requests</h2>

### When a Supplemental Notice Arrives

A supplemental request is not a rejection — it's a request to fill gaps — and adding the right materials within the deadline keeps the case moving.

The trouble starts when applicants take the supplemental notice literally and resubmit the same documents.

In reality, you need to read the intent behind what the notice lists and reinforce that part with a written explanation.

### Refiling After a Rejection

If a rejection comes through, refiling immediately on the same grounds is not advisable.

If the rejection was about financial capacity, strengthen the income-flow records; if it was about proving the family relationship, add further official documents — and then choose the right timing.

You can check the scope of available responses by reason against the Immigration Control Act, its Enforcement Decree, and Enforcement Rule at the [Korea Law Information Center](https://www.law.go.kr).

<h2 id="section-7">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

**Q1. Can I invite both parents at the same time?**
Whether you can depends on the inviter's status and whether the financial-capacity threshold is met.
In some cases, inviting one parent first has a higher approval rate, so the sequence needs to be designed.

**Q2. Is a Certificate of Visa Issuance required for F-3 dependent status?**
It isn't mandatory, but for accompanying-family cases with lots of Korean-side documents, the certificate route reduces the burden of supplemental requests.

**Q3. After receiving the certificate, can the visa be applied for late at the home-country mission?**
The certificate has a validity period and must be submitted to the home-country mission within that window.
Once it lapses, you have to reapply, so scheduling comes first.

**Q4. Can a marriage migrant (F-6) invite parents from their home country?**
Yes, conditionally — the scope widens when no other supporter exists in the home country, or when there are circumstances such as old age or illness.

**Q5. Does weak financial capacity always mean rejection?**
No.
There are cases that have been approved by reinforcing with co-supporters, assets, and housing stability.
But which form of reinforcement fits your case is determined in consultation.

**Q6. Can I switch the certificate-in-process to a different family member?**
Changing the invitee is treated as a separate matter and requires a new application.
